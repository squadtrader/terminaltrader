Paulson’s comments add a voting member’s voice to the hawkish chorus after the hot CPI report, which reinforces pricing for a December hike and a follow-up in January. Her emphasis on gradual steps tempers the case for a half-point move, but her three triggers for faster action leave that door open if data keeps running hot. Earlier:

---

Paulson favours steady hikes rather than sprints, but has set out exactly what would make her pick up the pace.

Summary:

Philadelphia Federal Reserve President Anna Paulson said on Tuesday that further gradual interest rate increases appear appropriate to bring down elevated inflation, and that she would favour moving faster if price pressures worsen.

In the text of a speech prepared for a gathering in Conshohocken, Pennsylvania, Paulson said monetary policy needs to be somewhat restrictive to return inflation to the Fed’s 2% target. Inflation has run above that goal for more than five years, she noted, with underlying pressures holding between 2.5% and 3%. She said the exact path of rates will depend on how conditions evolve and that she will be watching the data closely.

Paulson, a voting member of the Federal Open Market Committee this year, argued that a gradual approach has served the Fed well. In her view, it gives the economy time to adjust to higher borrowing costs while allowing policymakers room to assess the effects of their decisions. She said she has supported the Fed’s recent rate decisions and described bringing inflation down as her main priority, with the economy proving stronger than expected and the job market robust and stable.

She also set out the conditions under which she would favour a quicker pace. Paulson said she would want to act more quickly if inflation expectations drift higher, if wage pressures build, or if underlying inflation rises. She described risks to the inflation outlook as high and said the effects of AI and energy on prices are hard to gauge.

Her remarks came after October consumer price data showed a larger-than-expected rise, with the overall index up 0.4% on the month. Paulson said the report suggests inflation pressures remain very much present, pointing to tariffs, energy prices and AI investment as drivers. Economists saw signs that price increases once concentrated in a few areas are spreading more broadly.

Futures markets are pricing strong odds of a quarter-point rate hike at the Fed’s December 8-9 meeting, from the current target range of 3.75% to 4%, and a further increase in late January. Before the CPI release, Governor Christopher Waller said a reading like October’s could lead him to support a half-point move. The December meeting is now the key test of whether the Fed sticks with gradual steps or speeds up.

================================================================================

Strong language like this from Goolsbee adds weight to the hawkish run of Fed commentary after the CPI report, and it strengthens pricing for a December hike. His focus on services and expectations points to concern about entrenched inflation, not transitory shocks, which raises the risk of a longer hiking cycle. That backdrop supports the US dollar and front-end Treasury yields, and remains a headwind for gold. With energy feeding the cost side of inflation, oil prices stay central to how hawkish the Fed may need to be.

---

Earlier:

---

Goolsbee has gone from cautious to alarmed: sticky services prices and broad overheating make inflation a problem the Fed now has to confront.

Summary:

Chicago Federal Reserve President Austan Goolsbee said Tuesday’s consumer price report was “borderline disturbing”, warning in an interview with PBS NewsHour that the US has a real inflation problem that policymakers will have to address.

Goolsbee said the most troubling element of the October data was that services inflation is not coming down. Services prices tend to reflect domestic demand and wage costs more than goods prices do. A lack of progress there suggests price pressures are becoming more entrenched rather than fading as one-off shocks unwind.

He described inflation as being driven from both the cost side and the demand side. His fear, he said, is the emergence of overheating across a broad range of prices, rather than increases confined to a few categories. At the same time, he noted that the job market remains steady and stable, which removes one of the usual arguments for holding back on tighter policy.

Goolsbee said a key concern would be inflation becoming built into expectations, and that the Fed would have to come to terms with it if price pressures stay persistent. He also pointed out that business investment is affected by interest rates, suggesting he is weighing how higher borrowing costs could feed through to spending. He stressed that everything is always on the table and that the Fed must bring inflation down to its target.

The interview added to a run of hawkish commentary from Fed officials after October’s CPI showed a larger-than-expected increase. Earlier on Tuesday, Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson said further gradual rate hikes seem appropriate, while Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman said she was shifting her focus to inflation. Markets are pricing strong odds of a quarter-point hike at the Fed’s December 8-9 meeting.

Goolsbee’s choice of language stands out for an official who has often struck a more measured tone. With services inflation sticky and the labour market holding up, December’s decision will show how far policymakers are prepared to go to stop inflation becoming entrenched.

TITRE : NIESR sees UK inflation above BoE target for four years, peaking at 4.4% in 2027
DATE  : 11-11-2026 00:09 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/niesr-sees-uk-inflation-above-boe-target-for-four-years-peaking-at-4-4-in-2027/
================================================================================

A longer inflation overshoot driven by the Iran war’s energy shock keeps oil prices at the centre of the UK rate outlook: every sustained rise in crude raises the risk of further second-round effects on wages. NIESR’s call for only one more hike contrasts with market pricing of two or three. Its view that rates may need to reach around 5% points the other way, and supports the case for gilt yields and sterling to stay sensitive to energy-led data surprises. The critique of the October budget adds fiscal credibility risk on top, which could weigh on longer-dated gilts if borrowing plans keep slipping.

---

The Bank of England not the only one in deep doggy-doo:

---NIESR sees a longer inflation overshoot in Britain, and a BoE that may be doing less than the job requires.

Summary:

UK inflation will stay above the Bank of England’s 2% target for the next four years as the Iran war’s energy shock feeds through into wages, the National Institute of Economic and Social Research (NIESR) forecast on Tuesday. The think-tank also raised its outlook for growth.

It now expects inflation to peak at 4.4% in February 2027, up from the 3.8% peak it forecast in July. It sees inflation remaining above target through 2027, 2028 and 2029 before returning to 2% in late 2030, a year later than it previously expected. Second-round effects on wages are the main reason for the delay.

The outlook for policy is where NIESR parts company with markets. Investors are pricing two or three quarter-point rate hikes by the end of next year, but the think-tank expects the BoE to raise rates only once more, in February. NIESR also said rates would need to rise to around 5% to bring inflation back to target over the medium term, which suggests it thinks a single hike would not be enough to meet the goal on time. The BoE raised Bank Rate to 4% from 3.75% last week, its first increase in more than three years.

NIESR Director David Aikman said the economy had held up better than expected this year but now faces the twin pressures of higher energy prices and rising global borrowing costs. He said the policy response on both the monetary and fiscal sides is a domestic choice, and that decisions on both fronts are being put off.

On the fiscal side, NIESR said finance minister John Healey’s October budget did little to strengthen the public finances. Difficult decisions on tax and spending had largely been deferred, it said, and plans to reduce borrowing lacked credibility.

Growth has been more resilient than expected. NIESR raised its 2026 forecast to 1.6% from 1.1%, citing stronger government spending and business investment, partly driven by AI. It expects growth to slow to 1.1% in 2027 and 0.9% in 2028. Unemployment is projected to edge up from 4.8% in 2026 to a peak of 5.1% in 2031, with wage growth averaging 3.9% over the next three years.

The gap between NIESR’s single-hike forecast and market pricing will be tested at the BoE’s next meetings, with energy prices and wage settlements the key data to watch.

---

NIESR is Britain’s longest-established independent economic research institute. It was founded in 1938 and is based in London. It is a non-profit charity, independent of government and political parties, and it publishes regular forecasts for the UK and global economies, which are widely watched alongside those from the Bank of England and the government’s fiscal watchdog. Its director, David Aikman, joined in 2023 from the Bank of England, where he worked on financial stability.

================================================================================

A tier-one bank moving to three hikes in the cycle supports the US dollar and front-end Treasury yields, and adds to pressure on rate-sensitive equities and gold. Pushing the first cut back to December 2027 extends the period of restrictive policy, which could weigh on long-duration assets. The reversal of Goldman’s late-October call also shows how much oil is driving the outlook: the Middle East escalation it flagged as a risk has fed directly into the inflation data. Further gains in crude, including from attacks on Hormuz shipping and Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, would raise the odds of the hike coming in January rather than March.

---

Three hot inflation prints have turned Goldman’s “maybe” on a March hike back into a “likely”.

Summary:

Goldman Sachs now expects the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates again in March 2027, following a quarter-point hike in December, after a third consecutive month of hotter-than-expected inflation data, the bank’s chief US economist David Mericle said in a note on Tuesday.

Goldman continues to forecast a 25 basis point increase at the Fed’s December 8-9 meeting, which would be the second hike of the current tightening cycle. The new March call adds a third. Mericle said the move could be brought forward to January, but March is slightly more likely. Inflation readings for November and December are expected to be relatively mild, while larger price increases early in the year are likely to lift January’s figures. The bank also pushed back its forecast for the first rate cut by a quarter, to December 2027.

The change follows October’s consumer price report, which showed a larger-than-expected rise. Goldman expects the run of firm inflation data to lead most Fed policymakers to signal a third hike in the December dot plot, and said a strong January inflation report would make delivering that hike the path of least resistance. The bank remains confident, however, that inflation will be meaningfully lower by the end of 2027 as the effects of tariffs, the energy shock and AI-related price pressures fade.

The forecast reverses a call Goldman made only weeks ago. In late October, the bank had removed a March hike from its base case. It warned at the time that an escalation in the Middle East that drove oil prices higher, combined with persistent inflation, could change the Fed’s path. Its asset management arm has also cautioned that hotter inflation could force a more aggressive tightening cycle extending into 2027.

Goldman’s revised outlook is broadly in line with Fed commentary this week. Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson said further gradual hikes seem appropriate, Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee called the CPI report borderline disturbing, and Governor Christopher Waller had said a hot reading could lead him to back a larger move. Markets are pricing a quarter-point December hike as close to certain, with a small chance of a half-point move.

With the federal funds target range currently at 3.75% to 4%, December’s decision and its updated projections will show whether policymakers are ready to signal the further tightening Goldman expects.

TITRE : UBS sees limited support for a 50bp Fed hike in December despite hot October CPI
DATE  : 11-11-2026 23:49 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/ubs-sees-limited-support-for-a-50bp-fed-hike-in-december-despite-hot-october-cpi/
================================================================================

With futures pricing slightly more than a full quarter-point hike for December, the 50bp debate is now a tail risk the market will react to on any Fed speaker headline. Treasury yields close to 5% keep the dollar supported, including against the yen ahead of the Bank of Japan’s own expected December hike, which leaves the US-Japan rate gap a key driver of USD/JPY. Higher real yields are also a headwind for gold, which has tended to trade on Fed hike expectations rather than as a safe haven this year. For equities, the combination of firmer inflation and rising long-end yields adds pressure to richly valued tech shares, which are already dealing with the Nvidia leadership shock.

---

October’s inflation report makes a December Fed hike look all but certain, and UBS thinks the real risk is a faster run of quarter-point moves rather than one big step.

Summary:

A hotter than expected US inflation report has put an unusual question in front of the Federal Reserve: whether its next hike should be a double-sized one. UBS thinks the answer is still no. But the bank says October’s data strengthen the case for at least two more quarter-point increases and raise the risk that they come faster.

Headline consumer prices rose 0.4% in October, above the 0.3% forecast. Core prices, which exclude food and energy, rose 0.3% against expectations of 0.2%. Price gains were broad, covering food, housing, recreation, medical care, electricity, airfares and delivery services. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points to close to 5%, and the S&P 500 fell 0.4% on Tuesday.

The 50 basis point idea came from Fed Governor Christopher Waller. Before the release, he said a core reading of around 0.3% or more could justify considering a half-point move in December.

Separately, Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan said she favors at least two more quarter-point hikes and suggested the Fed could move more quickly this winter.

Fed funds futures now price about 28 basis points of tightening for December. That implies a full quarter-point hike and only a small chance of something larger.

UBS argues that appetite on the rate-setting committee for a 50 basis point move is limited. The bank said a hike of that size could signal that policy is well below where it needs to be and make the tightening cycle look open-ended. Its base case remains quarter-point hikes in December and March.

The detail behind the headline numbers explains the concern. Monthly core inflation edged down to 0.30% from 0.33%, but the three-month annualized pace rose to about 3.75%, from below 2% as recently as August. Excluding used cars, which fell 1.2%, core goods prices rose around 0.4%. Tariff-sensitive items such as toys, furniture and bedding, and appliances each rose more than 1%, and IT hardware prices climbed 0.4%. UBS noted that those categories carry larger weights in the Fed’s preferred PCE gauge than in CPI. It said goods disinflation looks a less reliable theme heading into 2027.

Shelter inflation slowed to 0.30% from 0.39% but remains sticky. Energy rose a further 0.7%, although its contribution to the headline figure faded. After two disappointing reports in a row, the December debate is now about the size of the move, not whether one comes.
