Summary:

The Asian session brings a cluster of events that will test how firmly markets are leaning into a global tightening story. Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson speaks late in the US day, Japan’s national inflation figures for October are released at the same time, and China’s monthly loan prime rate fixing follows early in the session. The first two fit squarely into a hawkish narrative on both sides of the Pacific. The third is a largely procedural event that matters less than it used to.

Fed’s Paulson: a 2026 voter on a committee leaning toward hikes

Paulson is due to speak at 23:30 GMT (18:30 US Eastern) at a Money Marketeers of New York University dinner in New York. She is scheduled to address communications and monetary policy, and a speech text and a question-and-answer session are expected. As a 2026 FOMC voter, her views carry weight heading into the December 8 to 9 meeting, with the decision due on December 9.

Policy needs to be somewhat restrictive to get inflation back to 2%, she said, and underlying inflation appears stuck between 2.5% and 3%. The key question for Paulson is whether she sticks with “gradual” or shows any openness to the larger step Waller has floated. Any change in how she talks about the pace would make the headline.

Japan’s October CPI: inflation data ahead of a likely BoJ hike

Japan’s national CPI for October is due at 23:30 GMT (18:30 US Eastern). The consensus for the core measure, which excludes fresh food, is 2.3%, up from 2.1% in September. The prior headline reading was 2.1%, and the measure excluding both fresh food and energy was 2.4%. Economists have linked much of the recent pickup to higher energy costs from the Middle East conflict reaching household bills, while a weak yen has pushed up import prices.

The data arrive with the Bank of Japan in a hawkish mood. The policy rate is 1.25% after a hike in September, its highest in 31 years. On Thursday, Governor Kazuo Ueda said the bank expects to keep raising rates because monetary conditions remain accommodative. A Reuters poll shows 86% of economists expect a hike to 1.50% at the December 17 to 18 meeting, which would be a third increase this year. PBoC loan prime rates: an 18th hold expected for a fading benchmark

The LPR is compiled monthly from quotes submitted by 20 designated commercial banks. The one-year rate is the benchmark for most corporate and household loans. The five-year rate is the main reference for mortgage pricing, which makes it the rate to watch for any property support.

As a policy signal, though, the LPR has slipped down the rankings. The PBoC made the seven-day reverse repo its main policy rate in 2024 (most recently at 1.40%), and has now gone a step further. From November 2, overnight reverse repo operations (at 1.25%) became the central bank’s main daily operation, and seven-day operations are now a liquidity tool used as needed. Governor Pan Gongsheng had said in October that the interbank overnight repo rate had effectively become the PBoC’s target. The switch lowered the floor of the rate framework by 15 basis points, although analysts stress it is a reform rather than a rate cut. The PBoC also plans to let stronger firms price loans off the overnight rate instead of the LPR, which would weaken the LPR’s role further.

Summary:

China left its benchmark lending rates unchanged for an 18th consecutive month on Friday. The one-year loan prime rate stayed at 3.0% and the five-year rate at 3.5%, in line with expectations from most market participants.

A Reuters survey of 28 respondents had found 23 expecting no change. Five forecast a cut to one or both rates, the first break from unanimous hold calls in recent months. That minority view reflected signs of renewed weakness in October, with factory output slowing and consumption subdued. Citigroup had described a November cut as implausible and expects rates to stay on hold through the rest of 2026, followed by two 10 basis point cuts in 2027.

What the LPR is

The loan prime rate is a benchmark lending rate set once a month. Twenty designated commercial banks submit quotes to the People’s Bank of China, and the published rate is meant to reflect what banks charge their most creditworthy borrowers. Other loans are priced as a spread above it.

What the two rates are used for

The one-year LPR is the reference for most corporate and household loans, including working capital lending to businesses. The five-year LPR is the main benchmark for mortgages, which makes it the rate to watch for any support aimed at the property sector.

Why it matters less than it used to

The LPR is not the PBoC’s policy rate. Banks set their quotes based on the central bank’s own operations, so the LPR tends to follow policy moves rather than signal them. For years, the medium-term lending facility played that anchoring role. In 2024 the PBoC made the seven-day reverse repo rate its main policy rate, most recently at 1.40%.

The bank has now gone a step further. From November 2, overnight reverse repo operations at 1.25% became the PBoC’s main daily operation, with seven-day operations kept as a liquidity tool used as needed. Governor Pan Gongsheng said in October that the interbank overnight repo rate had effectively become the central bank’s target. Analysts stress the switch is a reform rather than a rate cut, even though it lowered the floor of the rate framework by 15 basis points.

The PBoC also plans to let financially strong companies price loans off the overnight rate rather than the LPR. Over time, that would weaken the LPR’s role even in its core job of pricing bank loans.

For markets, that means a monthly LPR hold tells investors little on its own. Any shift in China’s policy stance is now more likely to show up first in the overnight rate, in reserve requirement changes or in targeted lending tools, with the LPR following later.
