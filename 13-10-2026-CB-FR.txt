The daily fixing is Beijing's quietest but clearest tool for telling markets how fast it wants the yuan to move, and after months of saying slow down, it has lately been saying go, gradually.

Summary:

The People's Bank of China sets the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT, a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets. It has taken on added significance in recent weeks as policymakers have shifted from restraining the yuan's rise to guiding it higher.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the yuan is allowed to trade within a band of plus or minus 2% around the central midpoint set each trading day. That midpoint reflects a mix of inputs, including the previous day's closing price, movements in major currencies such as the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic considerations like capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The calculation is not purely mechanical, giving policymakers room to use the fixing to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is set, onshore USD/CNY trades freely within the allowable range. Should market pressure push the currency toward either edge of that band, the central bank can step in to smooth volatility, whether through direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance channelled through state-owned banks. As a result, the daily fixing is often read as a policy signal rather than a purely technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected midpoint typically suggests the PBOC is leaning against depreciation, while a weaker fixing can signal tolerance for a softer currency, or unease with how quickly the yuan is rising.

That second reading has dominated for much of the past year. For nearly a year, the central bank set its daily guidance weaker than market expectations, a pattern traders and analysts interpreted as an attempt to slow the yuan's appreciation. The gap reached its widest since February in late August, shortly after the yuan touched a three-and-a-half-year high against the dollar.

September has brought a clear change of tone. The midpoint has strengthened at a faster pace this month, narrowing its gap with market expectations and suggesting the PBOC has eased its resistance to yuan gains. The central bank strengthened the fixing for eight consecutive sessions, the longest streak since 2023, and the midpoint has since reached its strongest level since February 2023. The onshore yuan has traded at its strongest since January 2023, while the offshore rate has reached levels last seen in July 2022. Even so, the fixing is still being set well weaker than market estimates, with one recent midpoint more than 500 pips below a Reuters survey forecast, a reminder that Beijing is allowing appreciation on its own terms rather than stepping aside.

The underlying tension is a familiar one. A firmer yuan supports capital stability and eases import costs, but a rapid rise risks undermining export competitiveness at a moment when China's broader economy continues to show signs of softness. For traders and investors, the gap between each day's fixing and consensus estimates remains one of the clearest windows into how Beijing is weighing that trade-off, and whether the recent green light for gains stays in place once the summit has passed.

The minutes read hawkish,not unexpectedly,with the board explicitly keeping further hikes on the table and markets already pricing roughly another 15 basis points by year-end and 30 basis points in the first half of 2027. Oil is central to the outlook: domestic fuel prices were running well above the RBA’s August assumptions, and any further escalation of the Iran war would feed directly into the risk of more tightening. ---

The RBA has concluded the economy is running hotter and the neutral rate is higher than it thought, which is central bank language for “we may not be done yet”.

Summary:

The Reserve Bank of Australia raised interest rates last month because several of the upside risks to inflation it had flagged in August had either materialised or were building, according to minutes of the 28 to 29 September meeting released on Tuesday. The board signalled it was prepared to tighten further if needed.

The board voted unanimously to lift the cash rate by 25 basis points to 4.60%. Members said the August forecasts, which had inflation returning to the midpoint of the target range by around late 2027, depended on a series of favourable developments that no longer looked likely to all occur.

The minutes set out three main arguments for the move. First, the risks identified in August had largely come to pass. The Middle East conflict had broadened and oil prices were well above the level assumed in the forecasts, with domestic petrol and diesel prices running about 13% higher than projected. Global cost pressures were intensifying, driven partly by extraordinary demand for AI-related products, and domestic capacity appeared tighter than assessed. Second, members judged that responding quickly would best balance their inflation and employment goals, given inflation has been above target for an extended period and surveys point to a risk that business inflation expectations are rising. Third, the board said the cash rate might not be high enough to restore balance within a reasonable timeframe even if the risks did not fully materialise.

That last point reflected a significant shift in thinking about the neutral interest rate, the level at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. Members said that if market signals of a higher global neutral rate were correct, the previous 4.35% cash rate was less restrictive than it had been two years earlier. They added that if the RBA held still while other central banks tightened, Australian financial conditions could loosen through a weaker currency.

On the economy, the board noted June quarter growth was a little stronger than expected, driven by business investment that rose more than 10% over the year, partly reflecting data centre and renewable energy construction. Household consumption slowed to a below-trend pace, house prices fell faster than expected, and unemployment rose to 4.6% in August. Staff increasingly questioned whether the unemployment rate consistent with target inflation is higher than previously thought, which would mean more pressure on capacity than estimated. Unit labour cost growth, driven by weak productivity rather than strong wages, also remained above levels consistent with the target.

Members acknowledged the trade-off between inflation and employment had worsened because of global cost pressures, and said returning inflation to target would require accepting slower growth in demand and jobs. The board will remain attentive to incoming data and said it will do what is necessary, including increasing the cash rate further if required.

--Greene’s message is simple: by the time inflation shows up in wages and prices, it will be too late, so the Bank should move before it gets there.

Summary:

Bank of England policymaker Megan Greene said UK monetary policy is not that restrictive, warning that waiting for second-round inflation effects to become visible before acting would leave the central bank responding too late.

Greene, an external member of the Monetary Policy Committee, said she sees the bigger risk as failing to be proactive enough in tackling inflation, rather than raising interest rates too soon. Second-round effects occur when an initial price shock, such as a rise in energy costs, feeds into wages and broader price-setting, making inflation more persistent.

Her comments reinforce her position as one of the more hawkish members of the committee. Greene voted for a quarter-point rate increase at the Bank’s July and September meetings, when she was among three members outvoted by a majority that preferred to keep rates on hold.

Greene has made similar arguments in recent days. Last week, she said she expected the UK to see some second-round effects from the current inflation cycle and voiced concern that wage growth of around 3.5% next year would be worrying, raising the risk that higher prices feed into pay deals and prompt firms to raise prices further.

She said the International Monetary Fund’s latest forecasts for the UK did not surprise her. The IMF raised its 2026 UK growth forecast to 1.3% this week, citing stronger AI-related investment, while warning that the global outlook faces significant risks from the war in the Middle East and elevated energy prices.

The UK, like other major economies, is grappling with inflation pressure from higher oil and diesel prices following the conflict involving Iran. Policymakers who favour acting early argue that letting those costs become embedded in wages and inflation expectations would ultimately require more painful tightening later.

Markets have increasingly priced a rate hike at the Bank’s November meeting, and Greene’s remarks suggest she will continue to make the case for tighter policy. The next round of UK wage and inflation data will help determine whether that view gains broader support on the committee.

Earlier:

---

Collins sees a resilient economy as giving the Fed licence to keep tightening, with war-driven fuel costs the clearest reason inflation won’t get back to target on its own.

Summary:

Boston Federal Reserve President Susan Collins said on Tuesday she expects the US central bank will need to lift interest rates again before the end of 2026, arguing that further tightening is needed to return inflation to the Fed’s 2% target in a timely and lasting way.

In prepared remarks for a banking conference at the Boston Fed, reported by Reuters, Collins said she had supported the Federal Open Market Committee’s decision last month to raise its policy rate by a quarter of a percentage point to a range of 3.75% to 4.00%. She indicated that a modest additional increase would likely be appropriate this year, while stressing that her view on both the need for and timing of further action would rest on the full body of incoming data and how it shapes the balance of risks.

Collins framed the inflation fight as the Fed’s central task. With growth holding up and the labour market broadly balanced, she said policymakers have room to concentrate on bringing inflation back to target, a goal the Fed has now missed for more than five years. She described the risk of persistently high inflation as elevated and the central bank’s main focus at present.

Her baseline outlook was largely constructive. In what she called the most likely scenario, the economy keeps growing at a healthy pace, the job market stays close to full employment and inflation eases gradually back to 2%, helped by ongoing efficiency gains that keep supply and demand in balance. She added that a stable labour market should continue to underpin consumer spending.

Collins nonetheless identified three forces that could complicate that path: heavy investment in data centres and other artificial intelligence infrastructure, the continuing effect of tariffs, and higher energy costs linked to the war in the Middle East. She said she expects both headline and core inflation to run above target again in the coming months, pointing to the recent jump in prices for refined products such as jet fuel, diesel and heating oil.

Projections published at the September meeting already pencilled in another increase before year-end. Several Fed officials have signalled in recent weeks that the move is more likely to come in December than at the October 27-28 meeting, and Collins’ comments keep a further hike firmly on the table while leaving its timing open. She said the policy path into the new year would depend on the data.
