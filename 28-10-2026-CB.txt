Ahead:

---Bullock told senators that oil, AI-driven cost pressures and a tighter-than-thought labour market have pushed inflation above the RBA’s August track, making next week’s meeting a genuine question of whether 100bp of hikes is enough.

Summary:

Reserve Bank of Australia Governor Michele Bullock said inflation is likely to run higher than the central bank forecast in August, as a renewed surge in energy prices from the Middle East conflict compounds domestic capacity pressures, signalling an open question over further tightening when the RBA Board meets next week.

In her opening statement to the Senate Economics Legislation Committee on Wednesday, delivered shortly before the release of September quarter CPI data, Bullock said some of the upside risks to inflation that the RBA had flagged in August have materialised or are materialising. The RBA’s August forecasts had inflation returning to around the midpoint of its 2% to 3% target only in late 2027.

Energy is the main new source of pressure. Bullock said the conflict in the Middle East has re-escalated and global energy prices are now much higher than assumed in August. She said higher oil and related prices will add materially to inflation, both directly and as businesses facing higher transport costs try to pass them on, and noted that short-term inflation expectations are elevated. She also pointed to the global AI boom and extreme weather as sources of upward pressure on technology and agricultural prices.

Bullock stressed that inflation is not only being imported. Even though unemployment has risen a little more than expected, she said the labour market appears slightly tighter than the RBA had judged, citing elevated underlying inflation in areas that reflect domestic demand. Weak productivity growth means the economy cannot grow strongly without adding to price pressures, a challenge she described as fundamental for Australia and for real spending power over the next few years. She said a further period of subdued growth in aggregate demand is needed to bring inflation down sustainably.

The Board has raised the cash rate by 25 basis points since the committee’s June hearing, taking the cumulative increase this year to 100 basis points. Bullock said the full effects have yet to be felt, with household spending moderating only gradually and business investment picking up strongly on data centres and renewable energy projects. Housing prices have fallen in most capital cities, though they remain around 50% above early 2020 levels, and she said the RBA does not target house prices.

Looking ahead, Bullock said the Board will assess the data and the balance of risks next week, with a key question being whether the tightening so far will be enough to return inflation to target in a reasonable time. She added that higher rates are difficult for households but that high inflation cannot be allowed to persist, noting that central banks in many advanced economies are raising rates or signalling they will if needed.

TITRE : RBNZ holds OCR at 2.75% in surprise 4-3 vote, says tighter policy still likely
DATE  : 28-10-2026 01:08 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/rbnz-holds-ocr-at-2-75-in-surprise-4-3-vote-says-tighter-policy-still-likely/
================================================================================

Oil sits at the centre of the call: the RBNZ expects fuel to lift inflation to 4.4% in the December quarter, but the majority chose to look through the direct effects for now, betting that weak demand will limit pass-through. With a hike close to fully priced, the hold is likely to hit the New Zealand dollar and pull front-end yields lower. The 4-3 split and the guidance that tighter policy is still likely should limit how far December pricing unwinds. Policy divergence is now sharp.Hotter Australian core inflation this morningand ahawkish Bullockcontrast with a cautious RBNZ, which points to upside for AUD/NZD into the RBA meeting on November 2 and 3. The RBNZ’s warning about opaque AI financing and an abrupt global downturn adds a dovish tail risk that markets had largely ignored.

---

Background:

---

The RBNZ blinked: with a hike almost fully priced, four of seven members chose to wait, betting that weak demand will contain the fuel shock, while keeping December firmly in play.

Summary:

The Reserve Bank of New Zealand unexpectedly held its official cash rate at 2.75% on Wednesday, with the Monetary Policy Committee split 4-3, wrong-footing markets and every major local bank that had forecast a third consecutive hike. The committee said tighter policy is still likely to be needed, but that the timing remains conditional on the economic outlook.

Governor Anna Breman, Carl Hansen, Rebecca Williams and Prasanna Gai voted to hold, while Hayley Gourley, Paul Conway and Karen Silk voted for a 25 basis point increase to 3%. Markets had priced roughly a 90% chance of a hike ahead of the decision, and ANZ, ASB, BNZ, Westpac and Kiwibank had all expected a move.

The majority argued that the two hikes delivered in July and September give the committee time to assess their effects. Annual CPI inflation eased to 3.7% in the September quarter from 4.1% in June, lower than the RBNZ had expected, as the indirect effects of higher fuel prices proved smaller than assumed. These members noted that wage growth remains subdued, medium-term inflation expectations are stable and there is little evidence so far that businesses are using higher costs to widen their margins. They also stressed the risk of a weak recovery in household spending and investment.

The minority took a more hawkish view. They argued that core inflation has been above levels consistent with the target midpoint for some time, raising the risk that inflation becomes entrenched in price setting as the Middle East conflict and higher freight and shipping costs persist. With aggregate growth proving resilient, they said acting now would guard against sustained price pressure and could reduce the need for larger rate increases later, favouring a move to around the midpoint of the neutral range.

Fuel remains central to the outlook. The RBNZ said fuel prices have risen further because of ongoing disruption in global energy markets, including lost refining capacity and shipping problems, while the New Zealand dollar’s trade-weighted index has fallen around 3% since early September. It expects annual inflation to rise to 4.4% in the December quarter, largely on the basis of current oil futures prices, but cautioned that near-term forecasts are highly uncertain given volatility in oil and refined fuel prices. Excluding vehicle fuels, annual inflation rose to 3.0%, and core measures have climbed to around the top of the 1% to 3% target band. Price increases have also broadened, with 56% of the CPI basket, by weight, rising by more than 3% over the year.

The domestic economy is recovering unevenly. Export-exposed regions are benefiting from high commodity prices, strong goods demand and rising tourist numbers, while household consumption remains weak, employment growth is subdued and house prices continue to fall. The household saving rate is estimated at 3.7%, well above its 10-year average of 1%, suggesting consumers remain cautious.

The committee also flagged growing global risks. It said financial stability risks have increased since September, citing high debt levels and opaque, complex financing arrangements for some AI investments, and warned that rising long-term interest rates could expose vulnerabilities and that a global downturn could occur abruptly. Two members, Gai and Breman, said that risk should be factored into judgements about the neutral rate, the level at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains the economy, rather than relying on midpoint estimates.

The RBNZ made clear its next move could go either way. Members said information over the next six weeks could change the appropriate stance in either direction. Further tightening could be warranted if domestic price pressures fail to ease or broaden beyond what costs and normal pass-through can explain, while subdued pressures could justify holding. A sharp global downturn could even lead to easing.

The next decision comes with the December Monetary Policy Statement, which will include updated forecasts. Breman holds a media conference at 3pm New Zealand time, which is 02:00 GMT.

================================================================================

By saying it is hard to completely look through supply shocks, Bullock signalled that the RBA is less willing to ignore the energy-driven jump in prices than theRBNZ was earlier. Any further rise in crude therefore adds directly to the case for an RBA hike. Her blunt language (morehere earlier), together withfirmer core inflationand her view that demand still exceeds supply, supports pricing for a move on November 2 and 3, and backs the Australian dollar and front-end yields. The contrast with the RBNZ’s hold sharpens the policy divergence, whichfavours AUD/NZD. Bullock’s refusal to give guidance leaves some uncertainty, but the overall tone tilts clearly hawkish.

---Bullock didn’t pre-announce a hike, but “far too high” and “difficult to look through supply shocks” are about as close as a central banker gets without saying it.

Summary:

Reserve Bank of Australia Governor Michele Bullock said inflation is far too high, as she and her senior colleagues responded to hotter-than-expected September quarter CPI data during a Senate committee hearing on Wednesday, days before the RBA Board meets to decide on interest rates.

Bullock said the latest figures were consistent with the upside risks to inflation the RBA had flagged, and that it is difficult to completely look through supply shocks such as the current surge in energy prices. She added that domestic demand is still running ahead of the economy’s capacity to supply it, a sign that price pressures are not only being imported. She declined, however, to give guidance on the Board’s decision on November 2 and 3.

Her deputy, Andrew Hauser, said the CPI numbers were in line with expectations but still too high, while Assistant Governor Sarah Hunter said inflation came in stronger than the RBA had expected in August. The two comments are consistent: the data broadly matched what economists forecast ahead of the release, but ran above the path in the RBA’s August Statement on Monetary Policy. Hunter also said she does not expect Australia to fall into recession.

The remarks followed a data release that added to the RBA’s concerns. The trimmed mean, the central bank’s main gauge of underlying inflation, rose 1.0% in the September quarter and 3.7% year on year, above the 3.6% expected, while monthly headline inflation climbed to 4.3% in September. Earlier in the hearing, before the data was released, Bullock had said inflation was likely to run higher than the RBA’s August forecasts, citing re-escalating conflict in the Middle East, much higher energy prices and a labour market that looks a little tighter than the RBA had judged.

Bullock’s comment on supply shocks is significant. Central banks often look through one-off jumps in prices such as fuel, on the basis that they fade on their own. By saying that is hard to do completely, Bullock signalled that the RBA is concerned about higher energy costs spreading into broader prices and into inflation expectations, particularly with underlying inflation already above target.

The RBA has raised its cash rate by a cumulative 100 basis points this year. Bullock has said a key question for next week’s meeting is whether that tightening will be enough to return inflation to the 2% to 3% target in a reasonable time. While she stopped short of signalling a move, the combination of firmer core inflation, a tight labour market and the Governor’s blunt language keeps another hike firmly in play. The Board’s decision next week will be accompanied by updated forecasts.

TITRE : The full statement from the Bank of Canada rate decision for October 2026. Hikes by 25 basis points
DATE  : 28-10-2026 13:45 UTC

================================================================================

FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE

Media Relations

Ottawa, Ontario

October 28, 2026

Share this page on Facebook

Share this page on X

Share this page on LinkedIn

Share this page by email

The Bank of Canada today increased its target for the overnight rate by 25 basis points to 2.5%, with the Bank Rate at 2.75% and the deposit rate at 2.45%.

Since the JulyMonetary Policy Report, global economic activity has remained resilient, despite higher energy prices. In the United States, the economy continues to grow at a robust pace, supported by consumer spending and AI-related investment. Growth in the euro area has been resilient and broad-based. After slowing in the second quarter, Chinese growth rebounded in the third quarter, owing to strength in exports and a pickup in fiscal spending. With persistently elevated oil prices and refinery margins, inflation has risen in most countries.

Given the ongoing resilience of the global economy, the Bank has revised up global GDP growth slightly to 3.1% in 2026, while growth in 2027 and 2028 remains unchanged from the July projection at 3.2%.

Global financial conditions have tightened since July. Advanced economy sovereign bond yields are higher. Equity and credit markets have been volatile due to uncertainty about the outlook for the AI sector. The Canadian dollar had weakened somewhat on US-dollar strength but has since recovered to around 71 cents – the same as assumed in the July Report.

Growth picked up in Canada through the middle of the year. GDP growth was 3.3% in the second quarter, and recent indicators point to growth of about 2% in the third quarter. This is stronger than expected in the July Report. Consumer spending has been solid, even with slow population growth. Business investment rebounded, reflecting oil and gas sector activity and spending on machinery and equipment. Despite ongoing weakness in sectors affected by US tariffs, exports overall have strengthened, owing to robust US demand, higher energy production and increasing signs that businesses are adapting to the changing trade environment. The unemployment rate has been around 6½% for several months. A broad set of indicators continue to point to some excess supply in the economy.

Owing to new US tariffs, growth is expected to slow in the fourth quarter of 2026, before picking up again in 2027. This rebound is supported by business investment, exports and fiscal measures. Consumption growth is expected to moderate and housing to remain relatively weak.

The Canadian economy is expected to grow 1% in 2026, and 2% in 2027 and 2028. This is a somewhat stronger overall growth projection than the July Report. This forecast is still subject to considerable uncertainty, particularly regarding Canada-US trade relations.

Given persistently high oil prices, CPI inflation has been around 3% since April and is expected to rise to about 3½% by the end of the year. Excluding gasoline, inflation was 2.2% in September and measures of core inflation remain close to 2%. But cost pressures are building, with high fuel prices raising the costs of production and transportation. Imported industrial and electronics input prices have also increased. While businesses have mostly absorbed higher input costs so far, Bank surveys suggest cost pass-through will increase over the next year. Near-term household and business inflation expectations have risen, while longer-term inflation expectations remain well anchored.

The conflict in the Middle East is now in its eighth month and there is no clear path to resolution. The combination of higher-for-longer energy prices and increased pass-through adds to the persistence of inflation in the projection. CPI inflation is expected to average 3% in 2027, a percentage point above the July projection, then come down to 2% in the middle of 2028. The main sources of uncertainty around this forecast are developments in the Middle East and businesses’ responses to cost pressures.

In the outlook, inflation is higher and more persistent than in the July Report. Growth has picked up this year and is expected to recover further. Against this backdrop, Governing Council decided to increase the policy rate to reduce the risk that continuing high energy prices spread broadly to the prices of other goods and services. There could be a need to increase the policy rate further, but given uncertainties, Governing Council agreed to proceed carefully. The Bank remains committed to maintaining Canadians’ confidence in price stability.
