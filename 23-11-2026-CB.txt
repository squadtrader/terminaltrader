TITRE : SNB's Martin eyes second-round effects after inflation jumps to 1.5%, hike in view
DATE  : 23-11-2026 00:01 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/snb-s-martin-eyes-second-round-effects-after-inflation-jumps-to-1-5-hike-in-view/
================================================================================

Martin's focus on second-round effects is a hawkish signal, and it supports pricing for a December SNB hike. A tightening Swiss central bank alongside a hiking Fed changes the usual rate-differential story for the franc. Combined with strong safe-haven demand during the equity rout, that could keep the franc well bid, especially against the euro. That is the very currency risk Martin flagged, so the SNB may stay willing to lean against excessive franc strength even as it tightens. UBS shares remain exposed to headlines on the capital rules and any further talk of a headquarters move.

---

Swiss inflation is still within target, but its speed has the SNB watching wages and prices closely, and a December hike now looks like a live option.

Summary:

The Swiss National Bank is closely watching for second-round effects from rising inflation, Vice Chairman Antoine Martin said in an interview published on Sunday, adding to expectations of a rate increase in December. Speaking to Swiss newspaper Le Matin Dimanche, Martin said inflation had continued to climb since the central bank's last policy decision and that policymakers were paying particular attention to possible second-round effects.

His remarks follow a sharper-than-expected jump in Swiss annual inflation to 1.5% in October from 1% in September, which raised market expectations of a hike at the SNB's next meeting. Chairman Martin Schlegel said after the data that the jump had caught the bank by surprise. Inflation is still inside the SNB's 0% to 2% target range, but it seems to be the speed of the increase, not its level, that concerns officials. Second-round effects, where higher prices lead to bigger wage demands and wider price increases, are what can turn a temporary shock into more lasting inflation.

Martin also defended the franc's status as a safe haven. He said it "very clearly" still plays that role and that, in real terms, it is close to where it traded against the euro five years ago. The currency weakened against the dollar and euro earlier in the year, then strengthened sharply in October as AI-related market turbulence, higher energy prices and concerns over French public debt drove demand for safety. He described exchange-rate swings between the franc, dollar and euro as the central bank's main risk.

On banking regulation, Martin repeated the SNB's support for a government proposal that would require UBS to fully capitalise its foreign subsidiaries. The proposal is part of the regulatory overhaul that followed the collapse of Credit Suisse in 2023. UBS has called the plan excessive and a threat to its competitiveness, fuelling speculation it could move its headquarters abroad. Martin said any such move would be for the bank's management to decide, while pointing to Switzerland's political stability, predictable legal framework and skilled workforce.

The next test is the December policy decision. How the SNB weighs fast-rising inflation against a franc that is again in demand will shape how far it is prepared to go.

TITRE : Ackman urges Fed to drop 2% inflation goal for 3%, warns 9 December hike risks recession
DATE  : 23-11-2026 01:36 UTC

================================================================================

Ackman's post adds to a growing chorus, now including the Treasury, arguing against further Fed hikes. That could feed hopes of a softer message on 9 December even if the hike itself goes ahead. Rate markets are unlikely to shift much on investor commentary alone. Any sign that Fed officials are open to slowing the pace, though, would weigh on the dollar and front-end yields and support equities and gold. His SpaceX example also points to strain in credit markets, so corporate bond spreads are worth watching as a gauge of how much tightening is already in the system.

---

Earlier:

---

Ackman wants the Fed to move the goalposts to 3%, but a central bank that changes its target mid-fight risks losing the credibility it needs to win it.

Summary:

Pershing Square chief executive Bill Ackman has called on the Federal Reserve to abandon its 2% inflation target in favour of 3%. He warned that the central bank is about to make another mistake at its 9 December meeting and risks pushing the US and global economies into recession if it keeps raising rates.

In a post on X, Ackman argued that financial conditions have already tightened sharply. He pointed to SpaceX, which he described as an investment-grade company, raising secured debt at an interest rate of around 8.3%, compared with below 6% on the unsecured bonds it sold at the time of its listing. He added that the recent slide in the stock market has also made equity capital materially more expensive.

His broader case is that the world has changed since the 2% goal was set. Ackman cited geopolitical risk and the need for higher global military spending, deglobalisation and tariffs, and the vast investment under way in AI infrastructure and the energy needed to power it. Against that backdrop, he said, "3% inflation is a more realistic ambition in the new world." He ended the post by inviting critics to tell him why he was wrong.

The intervention comes in a week of growing pushback against further tightening. On Sunday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned that the Fed risks a policy mistake if it overtightens. Goldman Sachs data also show US financial conditions recorded their sharpest weekly tightening since March 2020 after last week's equity rout. Even so, futures price a December hike as close to certain, and Goldman's own economists expect increases in December and March.

The counterargument is likely to centre on credibility. Raising the target while inflation is running above it could be seen as moving the goalposts. That risks loosening the long-term inflation expectations the Fed has spent decades anchoring, and could mean higher rates later, not lower ones. Policymakers have so far leaned the other way: Philadelphia Fed President Anna Paulson said last week that rates will need to rise again because inflation is too high.

With the 9 December decision approaching, the debate over how much tightening the economy can take, and what the Fed should be aiming for, is now playing out openly between policymakers, the Treasury and some of Wall Street's most prominent investors.

Summary:

The stock market was due a correction in technology shares and is likely to bounce back, according to David Kelly, chief global strategist at JPMorgan Asset Management. He also pushed back against expectations of aggressive Federal Reserve tightening.

In an interview with CNBC late last week, Kelly said the pullback had been driven by Nvidia's recent problems and wider technological disruption, which he acknowledged were unsettling some investors. Outside the technology sector, though, he saw little evidence of strong demand, pointing to flat new housing activity and weak spending on consumer basics. He also expects inflation pressure to ease. He estimated that falling gasoline prices alone will take around four-tenths of a percentage point off the annual rate of consumer price inflation in November.

On policy, Kelly expects the Fed to raise rates in December and then pause, not keep tightening. He described Governor Christopher Waller as a lonely voice in backing a 50 basis point hike, and said he doesn't believe most policymakers share that view. He argued that markets were building a hawkish story in the absence of clear forward guidance, and that with rates already close to neutral there's no reason for the Fed to be aggressive.

Waller's position has hardened over the past fortnight. His view is at the softer end of the range. Goldman Sachs economists expect hikes in both December and March, and futures price a December move as close to certain. The size of that move, and any signal on what comes next, will be the key tests for Kelly's one-and-done call.

TITRE : OCBC: AI rout becomes a growth shock as EM currencies take the dollar's strength
DATE  : 23-11-2026 02:23 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/ocbc-ai-rout-becomes-a-growth-shock-as-em-currencies-take-the-dollar-s-strength/
================================================================================

If OCBC is right, dollar strength will be most visible in high-yielding and externally exposed EM currencies. That makes the case for selective EM FX hedges even while the dollar softens against the majors. Carry trades funded in low-yielders are vulnerable to further unwinding if the AI sell-off resumes, which could add volatility to Asian currencies, especially those tied to chip exports. -The AI unwind has stopped being just a rates story, OCBC says, and emerging market currencies are paying for the dollar's resilience.

Summary:

The market shock driven by higher interest rates may have turned into a growth shock, according to OCBC Bank strategists. They said the unwinding of AI-related risk is now hitting equities, emerging market carry trades and metals at the same time.

In a report on Monday, the strategists said worries about the AI investment cycle have pushed down both stocks and US Treasury yields. As a result, the pressure from a strong US dollar has shifted onto emerging market currencies, not spread across the major currencies. In the near term, they expect the dollar to keep benefiting from the risk of a more hawkish Federal Reserve and from the strain that tighter global financial conditions put on the rest of the world.

The view marks a notable evolution for the Singapore bank. In mid-October, OCBC FX strategists Sim Moh Siong and Christopher Wong described AI as the dollar's potential weak spot. They argued that when a shock starts in the US, losses on US assets can outweigh the currency's usual safe-haven appeal. By early November they had turned more supportive, expecting the dollar to stay firm into the first half of 2027, with EUR/USD potentially dipping below 1.10 before recovering later in the year. At the time, they also cautioned that markets might start to question how far the Fed could tighten if AI-related stress led to a sharp tightening in financial conditions.

Monday's note suggests both forces are now in play. Financial conditions have tightened sharply: US equities had their worst week in years, and Goldman Sachs measured its biggest weekly tightening since March 2020. Yet the dollar's strength is showing up mainly against emerging market currencies, which are more exposed to capital outflows and funding stress. Korea illustrates the dynamic: the Kospi has come under heavy pressure and authorities have readied a market stabilisation fund.

The framing echoes Goldman Sachs strategist Ben Snider, who said last week that the sell-off had expanded from an AI unwind into a broader growth downgrade.

In early Asian trade on Monday, the dollar eased against major currencies as US stock futures bounced. OCBC's warning is that the relief may be uneven: as long as the Fed keeps its tightening bias and global conditions stay tight, emerging market currencies are likely to remain the pressure point.
