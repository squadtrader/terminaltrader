An AI-fuelled surge in business investment has pushed Australian demand beyond what the economy can comfortably supply, and Citigroup warns that the RBA, while likely on hold for now, cannot rule out more hikes.

Summary:

Australian business investment posted its biggest quarterly jump in 15 years in the third quarter, and Citigroup warns that the economy is now running too hot for the Reserve Bank of Australia to rule out further rate hikes.

Private business investment rose 12% from the previous quarter, the largest gain since the mining boom of 2011 and almost three times the consensus forecast of around 4%. Spending on equipment, plant and machinery did the heavy lifting, surging 23% on the back of strong AI-related investment.

Citigroup chief economist Josh Williamson said domestic demand may have grown by as much as 1.5% in the quarter, lifting annual economic growth to around 3.3%, a pace he describes as overheated. In his view, demand is now growing faster than the economy's potential growth rate, the speed at which it can expand without generating extra inflation. At the same time, the AI investment boom is competing with the rest of the economy for land, labour and capital, adding to pressure on resources that are already stretched.

That combination matters for monetary policy. When demand persistently outruns the economy's capacity to supply, prices tend to rise, which is the risk central banks try to head off. Williamson's base case is that the RBA will keep the cash rate on hold at 4.85%, but he sees a real risk that the bank will have to tighten further.

The investment surge also changes the shape of the growth story. A boom led by equipment and technology spending can lift the economy's productive capacity over time, which would eventually ease inflation pressure. In the near term, though, the build-out itself adds to demand for workers, materials and finance, putting it in direct competition with households and other businesses.

The data will feed into the debate over how close the RBA is to the end of its tightening cycle. For markets, the key question is whether strong investment proves a one-off burst tied to AI projects or the start of a sustained upswing in capital spending. A sustained upswing would make a prolonged hold harder to defend and keep the door open to further hikes.

Tokyo core inflation has climbed to its fastest pace in 16 months, strengthening the case for a December BoJ hike, though a slight easing in the bank's preferred trend gauge suggests energy did much of the lifting.

Summary:

Core inflation in Tokyo climbed to its highest in 16 months in November, keeping price growth well above the Bank of Japan's 2% target and reinforcing expectations that the central bank will raise interest rates next month.

Core consumer prices in the capital, which include fuel but exclude fresh food, rose 2.9% from a year earlier, according to data reported by Reuters on Friday. That was up from 2.6% in October and the fastest annual pace since July 2025, although it fell just short of the 3.0% median forecast. Core inflation has now held above the BoJ's target for three consecutive months.

The end of government utility subsidies accounted for part of the jump, but the increases were broad-based, with prices rising for food, daily necessities and hotel stays. One economist expects core inflation to keep accelerating as higher energy costs from the Middle East conflict feed through into other prices.

The picture is a little softer underneath. A measure that strips out both fresh food and fuel, which the BoJ watches closely as a gauge of trend inflation, eased slightly to 2.5% from 2.6% in October. That leaves underlying inflation comfortably above target, but suggests energy did much of the heavy lifting in the core rise.

Tokyo figures are widely seen as a leading indicator for the nationwide data. Those are due on the morning of December 18 in Tokyo, the final day of the BoJ's two-day policy meeting. That timing makes Friday's release one of the last major inflation readings that policymakers and markets can fully weigh before the decision.

Markets widely expect the BoJ to raise its policy rate to 1.5% from 1.25% at that meeting. The bank has already moved faster than in previous years, hiking in June and again in September, compared with roughly two hikes a year in 2024 and 2025. In October it held rates, but said underlying inflation had roughly reached its 2% target and signalled readiness to keep tightening. A majority of economists in a Reuters poll expect a December hike followed by another by the end of March, with many seeing rates peaking at around 1.75% or 2.0%.

Hawkish board member Naoki Tamura added to the pressure on Thursday, saying the bank must respond swiftly to signs of an inflation overshoot, including by speeding up its pace of hikes.

The main risk to a December move comes from markets. The same economist said the BoJ could hold off if instability linked to worries over AI and the chip sector persists, though a hike next month remains the baseline.

The IMF wants the RBA ready to tighten further if inflation sticks, even though its report was written before the November hike, and it is pressing the government to make sure fiscal policy pulls in the same direction.

Summary:

The International Monetary Fund has urged the Reserve Bank of Australia to stand ready to raise interest rates further if inflation proves more stubborn than expected, while pressing the government to follow through on its commitments to rein in spending.

In its 2026 Article IV consultation report on Australia, reported by Bloomberg on Friday, the IMF said further monetary tightening would be needed if upside shocks to inflation materialise, including if the war in the Middle East escalates and energy prices stay high for longer. It also warned that, after several years of inflation above target and a run of shocks, inflation expectations risk becoming less well anchored.

The report appears to have been finalised before the RBA's latest move. It refers to 100 basis points of cumulative tightening this year, but the central bank's November hike lifted that total to 125 basis points, taking the cash rate to 4.85%, the highest in 18 years. So the IMF's call for readiness to tighten further comes on top of a hike it had not yet accounted for.

On fiscal policy, the IMF described the shift towards consolidation as appropriate but said delivering on spending restraint is crucial. It said federal and state budgets for 2026/27 are rightly focused on controlling structural spending over the medium term, but more work is needed to lock in lasting savings. The message is that monetary policy should not be left to carry the inflation fight alone.

The IMF's stance lines up with the more hawkish voices on the RBA. Citigroup said this week that domestic demand is now growing faster than the economy's potential after a surge in AI-related business investment. It expects the RBA to hold at 4.85% but sees a risk of further hikes. Not everyone agrees. OCBC argued this week that the RBA is close to the end of its tightening cycle, pointing to a softening labour market, with unemployment at a five-year high of 4.9%.

The RBA's own board was split last time: six members voted for the November hike and three preferred to hold, underlining how finely balanced the decision has become. The bank's next policy announcement is due at 03:30 GMT on December 8 (22:30 US Eastern on December 7). With inflation still above the 2% to 3% target band and the IMF now explicitly backing further tightening if needed, the bar for a pause looks high, even as the jobs market cools.

The Reserve Bank of Australia hiked to 4.85% at its November meeting.

OCBC thinks the Australian dollar can bounce when risk appetite returns, but with the RBA close to done and the jobs market weakening, it sees Australia's rate advantage fading and the Aussie's upside increasingly capped.

The Australian dollar may struggle to stage a lasting recovery even if global risk sentiment improves, according to OCBC. The bank argues that a softening labour market and a Reserve Bank of Australia nearing the end of its tightening cycle will gradually erode the currency's rate advantage.

For the Australian dollar, that implies any rebound driven by improving risk sentiment is likely to lack staying power.

Not everyone sees the RBA as finished. The International Monetary Fund said on Friday that the central bank should stand ready to raise rates further if inflation proves persistent. Citigroup warned that surging AI-related business investment has pushed domestic demand above the economy's potential, keeping the risk of further hikes alive.

TITRE : Warsh's 2018 dilemma returns as an AI stock rout meets a Fed bent on taming inflation
DATE  : 27-11-2026 04:17 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/warsh-s-2018-dilemma-returns-as-an-ai-stock-rout-meets-a-fed-bent-on-taming-inflation/
================================================================================

The message for equity investors is that the bar for Fed support is much higher than in 2018. With core inflation at 3.3%, an orderly decline in AI-linked stocks is more likely to be treated as useful tightening than as a reason to pause. That keeps a December hike firmly priced and supports the dollar and short-dated Treasury yields. The key signal is the Fed's December projections, which are expected to show more hikes ahead. Credit markets are the trigger to watch: stress among heavily indebted AI-related borrowers would be far more likely to shift the Fed's stance than falling share prices alone. For gold, which pays no yield, a Fed still focused on hiking remains a headwind.

---

Kevin Warsh once urged the Fed to back off as stocks slid in 2018, but with inflation stuck well above target and AI demand running hot, his Fed looks far more willing to let a market rout do some of its work.

Summary:

The rout in AI-linked stocks is testing how much market turmoil the Federal Reserve is willing to tolerate in its fight against inflation, and so far the answer appears to be a lot. If the selloff deepens, Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh may have to choose between delivering on his tough talk on prices and relying on falling markets to do some of the tightening for him.

AsNick Timiraos reported in The Wall Street Journal (gated), Warsh has faced this dilemma before, from the outside. In December 2018, with stocks about 11% below their peak, he co-wrote a piece urging the Fed to pause its tightening. The Fed hiked anyway, the selloff accelerated into the worst December for stocks since 1931, and officials quickly signalled they were in no rush to move again.

The parallels with today are striking. Then, as now, a first-year Fed chair was raising rates into falling stocks and a stronger dollar, during a geopolitical shock and after a midterm setback for President Trump, who opposed tighter policy. The crucial difference is inflation. In 2018 it had only just reached the Fed's 2% target. Today it has been above target for more than five years, and Warsh, who took over in May, has said the Fed will not accept inflation that stays even modestly above 2%.

That leaves little room for a 2019-style retreat. Core PCE inflation, which excludes food and energy, rose to 3.3% in October from 3.1% in September, well up from a low of 2.6% in March 2025, before broad US tariffs took effect. Fed officials are increasingly worried that AI is driving a surge in demand that, unlike an oil or tariff shock, will not fade on its own. New York Fed President John Williams has said his main concern is the strength of AI-related demand rather than past supply shocks.

Some see the market slide as part of the cure rather than a problem. Lower stock prices are one of the channels through which higher rates slow spending. Former Boston Fed President Eric Rosengren put it bluntly: "If you want to slow down the economy, you have to slow down AI investment." Officials are also wary of reinforcing the "Fed put", the belief that the central bank will cushion every selloff.

That does not mean markets are irrelevant. A sustained decline would weigh on household spending and make it harder for companies to raise money, which could reduce how far the Fed needs to go. Williams has said a material and sustained change in financial conditions would affect his outlook, but he saw recent swings as volatility rather than a fundamental reassessment of AI. A disorderly selloff is the bigger worry, especially in credit. Michelle Bowman, the Fed's Vice Chair for Supervision, has said AI-related debt is on track to top $800 billion this year, with off-balance-sheet commitments estimated at more than three times that.

Investors overwhelmingly expect another hike at the December 8-9 meeting, after the Fed raised rates in September. The debate is over what comes next. Some officials could favour stopping after one more move if falling commodity prices ease inflation. Others take a more hawkish view. St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem has argued that three more hikes next year could be warranted, and Williams has not ruled out consecutive increases.

Warsh's own record offers mixed signals. He has long argued the Fed should pay more attention to market prices than to lagging data. Yet in October 2018 he said the Fed should not fret over a 10% drop in stocks, only to call for a pause ten weeks later. How he weighs a deeper rout against stubborn inflation may become the defining test of his early tenure.
