With Wall Street in correction and a December decision approaching, the Treasury is now openly arguing that the Fed, not the energy shock, is the bigger risk to the economy.

Summary:

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Sunday that the Federal Reserve could damage the economy if it pushes policy too far. In an interview with CBS's Face the Nation, he said many people believe the central bank "may be making a policy mistake if they over-tighten." He pointed to a run of hawkish comments from Fed governors in recent weeks and said financial conditions had tightened substantially over the past few months.

The remarks came as Wall Street slid into correction territory amid a broad sell-off in AI-linked stocks. Bessent gave several explanations for the decline. He said the selling began as Democrats gained ground in the polls and picked up speed after Election Day, arguing that the party's push to slow the AI buildout had unsettled investors. He also named the Fed's tone as a factor. Bessent did not name the officials he had in mind, and he did not say what decision he would like to see from the Fed in December.

Sunday's comments continue an argument Bessent has made throughout November. In a CNBC interview on 13 November, he said it would be a mistake for the Fed to hike too aggressively into an energy supply shock that he said was already easing, and he described recent inflation data as noisy. Speaking to CNBC again on 20 November, he urged policymakers not to back a larger rate rise. He noted that one governor who now favours a 50 basis point increase had supported a cut of a similar size within the past year, and he told the Fed to look forward rather than react to past data.

Bessent's comments reflect the Treasury's view, not that of the officials who set rates. The Fed raised its target range by a quarter point in September to 3.75% to 4.00% and kept it there last month. Some governors are now leaning towards further tightening. Bessent's case is that oil-led price pressure will fade on its own. Fed officials who disagree are more concerned about how long the energy shock keeps inflation high.

The Fed's next decision is 17 days away. Before then, markets will look at incoming inflation data, further Fed commentary and how deep the equity correction goes to judge whether a December hike stays on the table, and how large it might be.
