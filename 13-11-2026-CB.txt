================================================================================

UBS’s view is more hawkish than current market pricing, which has moved towards RBA cuts within months. That leaves front-end Australian bond yields exposed if upcoming inflation data run hot. For the Australian dollar, a longer restrictive period would support rate differentials, but the weak domestic growth outlook limits how much it can benefit. The February RBA meeting is now a live risk if quarterly core inflation stays near a 1% pace. UBS’s upbeat global outlook depends partly on a Middle East resolution bringing oil prices down, so a lasting oil shock would hurt both growth and the inflation outlook.

---UBS says the AI boom is giving Australia the inflation now and the productivity later, which means no RBA cuts until late 2027 and a private sector that will feel like it’s in recession.

Summary:

The AI investment boom is adding to Australia’s inflation problem before it delivers any productivity gains, and that is likely to keep the Reserve Bank of Australia’s policy restrictive well into 2027, according to UBS. The bank’s chief economist for Australasia, George Tharenou, toldausbizhe does not expect rate cuts until late 2027 or 2028. He said that if the RBA does move in the near term, another hike is more likely than a cut.

Tharenou said a recent speech by RBA Assistant Governor Sarah Hunter (RBA’s Hunter warns AI could trigger sharp asset repricing as boom adds to inflation) showed how seriously the central bank is taking the AI shock. The RBA estimates data centre investment has risen about tenfold in three years, and the pipeline of approved projects could see it double again. The eventual productivity gain could be anywhere from 0.1% to 2% a year, but so far there is little evidence of it. For now, Tharenou argued, AI is acting as a demand shock in an economy where capacity is already tight and inflation is too high. That raises the neutral interest rate and argues for tighter policy for longer.

That puts UBS at odds with markets, which turned dovish after the RBA’s last meeting and started pricing cuts within months. UBS’s central case is an extended hold. But Tharenou flagged the February meeting as a possible hike if inflation surprises to the upside, particularly if quarterly core inflation runs near a 1% pace. He noted that several board members voted against hiking last time, so it would probably take a clear inflation surprise to change their minds.

On growth, UBS expects Australia’s economy to slow to about 1.4% in 2027, below most forecasts and slightly under the RBA’s. AI investment could add up to half a percentage point and government spending remains strong, which makes a technical recession hard to bring about. But outside the AI boom, Tharenou expects the private sector to be close to flat, with falling house prices weighing on consumers and unemployment rising above 5%. In his view, it will feel like a recession even if the data don’t show one.

He also questioned the strength of official household spending figures, which have been growing at around 6% to 7% a year, while listed retailers’ trading updates point to growth close to flat. He expects the official numbers to slow sharply over the coming year.

UBS is more upbeat about the world economy, forecasting global growth of about 3.5% in 2027, above consensus. That depends on the AI boom continuing and a resolution in the Middle East bringing oil prices down.

TITRE : US Treasury bought yen in July, its first intervention since 1998, as yen nears 160 again
DATE  : 12-11-2026 23:43 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/us-treasury-bought-yen-in-july-its-first-intervention-since-1998-as-yen-nears-160-again/
================================================================================

The report confirms that the US is a willing partner in yen support, not just a bystander. That raises the risk for anyone betting heavily on dollar-yen above 160, a level that now looks well defended. Positioning is likely to turn more cautious as the pair approaches the summer’s intervention zone, and volatility could spike on any further rate checks. Rate expectations still favour the dollar, though, because the US economy is resilient and inflation is high. That means intervention may only slow the yen’s slide unless the BOJ follows through with a December hike to 1.5%.

---

Yesterday:

---

Washington has now confirmed that it put its own money behind the yen this summer. With the currency back near 160, the main question is whether Tokyo and Washington can hold that line again.

Summary:

The US Treasury bought Japanese yen in the third quarter, its first yen-buying intervention since 1998, while the Federal Reserve did not intervene in currency markets on its own account, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York said on Thursday. The New York Fed’s quarterly report on foreign exchange operations confirms that Washington acted alongside Tokyo this summer to stop a sharp slide in the Japanese currency, Reuters reported.

According to the report, the New York Fed bought about 79 billion yen, worth roughly $500 million, in the spot market on 31 July. It paid with euros from the Treasury’s Exchange Stabilization Fund, and said the purchase was made to counter what the Treasury saw as disorderly moves in the yen. Rate checks on dollar-yen had been reported on 30 July. These are calls to dealers asking for prices, and markets often read them as a sign that intervention is coming. The report said the New York Fed made those checks solely on the Treasury’s behalf, acting as its fiscal agent. The yen rose more than 2% that day.

Japan’s Ministry of Finance intervened at the same time, spending about 15 trillion yen during the quarter. The combined action worked quickly: between 30 July and 3 August, dollar-yen fell from around 164 to around 155. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in early August that the US would not hesitate to join further intervention, and repeated his call for the Bank of Japan to keep raising interest rates.

The yen’s recovery has not lasted. It has drifted back toward 160 per dollar, its weakest level since September. On Thursday, Atsushi Mimura, Japan’s vice finance minister for international affairs and its top currency diplomat, warned that it was too early to forget the summer’s intervention. Analysts at Mizuho Securities expect the dollar to keep rising against the yen, supported by a resilient US economy and inflation kept high by strong AI investment, and flagged the risk of fresh action by Japan.

The Bank of Japan is the other tool available. Some BOJ policymakers called in October for faster rate rises, arguing that underlying inflation has reached the 2% target. Most economists in a Reuters poll expect the bank to raise its policy rate to 1.5% in December, after hikes in June and September. Now that the US is on record as a willing partner, any renewed push toward 160 and beyond is likely to meet stronger resistance from both governments.

TITRE : Fed’s Williams says inflation stubbornly high and Fed still has work to do on policy
DATE  : 13-11-2026 00:06 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/fed-s-williams-says-inflation-stubbornly-high-and-fed-still-has-work-to-do-on-policy/
================================================================================

Williams’ remarks support pricing for a December hike and the dollar’s rate advantage, keeping front-end Treasury yields firm. His emphasis on acting “over time” and on a steady, data-led approach suggests no urgency for a half-point move, which may limit how far bets on a larger hike can build. The comments add to pressure on gold, which has been falling as hike expectations push up real yields. Higher-for-longer messaging also keeps oil-driven inflation risk at the centre of the policy outlook.

---

Williams says the Fed has more tightening to do but suggests a steady pace, which supports a December hike without adding much to bets on a bigger move.

Summary:

New York Fed President John Williams said inflation remains too high and the Federal Reserve still has work to do to get monetary policy to the right place, reinforcing expectations that the central bank will keep raising interest rates. Speaking on a panel at a UC Berkeley alumni event in New York on Thursday evening (2330 GMT), Williams described inflation as stubbornly persistent. He said the Fed will need to take action over time to bring it back to its 2% target.

Williams was confident about the economy. He said it has been resilient and growth has been strong, and that the US economy is getting stronger but is not overheating. That combination matters for the policy debate. A solid economy gives the Fed room to tighten further without tipping growth into reverse, while the absence of overheating suggests he does not see a need to rush.

He also said the Fed has been collecting data at a steady cadence, language that points to a methodical, meeting-by-meeting approach rather than a sudden change of pace. His comment that action will be needed “over time” suggests he supports continued, gradual tightening.

The remarks come two days after a hotter-than-expected October consumer price report. Headline prices rose 0.4% on the month and core prices 0.3%, both above forecasts. Since then, markets have priced a high probability of a quarter-point hike at the Fed’s 9 December decision, along with an outside chance of a half-point move. Fed Governor Christopher Waller has said he would favour a quarter-point hike in December and could prefer a larger step if inflation runs hotter than expected.

In the comments reported, Williams did not commit to a specific move in December. But he is a permanent voter and vice chair of the Federal Open Market Committee, so his views carry significant weight. His message is also in line with his remarks in Frankfurt a week earlier, when he called inflation far too high.

For markets, the comments support the case for a December hike without encouraging bets on a larger move. The next test is the data due before the meeting, especially further inflation readings, which will decide whether “over time” means one hike or several.

The Fed has seen the most significant hawkish repricing this week, driven  by Fed Governor Waller's  hawkish remarks and the hotter than expected US inflation data. Waller said he would support a 25 bps rate hike in December even if inflation improved, while warning that a 50 bps increase could be necessary if inflation surprised to the upside.

October CPI subsequently came in above expectations, with headline inflation rising 0.4% m/m vs 0.3% expected and core CPI increasing 0.3% vs 0.2% expected. The increased US-Iran tensions have added to inflation concerns through the energy channel, reinforcing the hawkish pricing.

The BoE saw a hawkish repricing after the better than expected UK GDP data and some hawkish remarks from MPC members. The BoE already raised rates by 25 bps to 4% at the last meeting, with an 8–1 vote. One member even favoured a larger 50 bps increase.

The main driver remains the inflation outlook. UK CPI accelerated to 3.5% in September, and the BoE expects inflation to approach 5% by mid-2027 as higher energy prices feed through the economy. The stronger than expected activity data and persistent domestic inflation pressures have also strengthened the case for further tightening. The prolonged US-Iran conflict just adds another layer of upside risk to inflation expectations.

The RBNZ unexpectedly kept rates unchanged at 2.75% in October, in a 4–3 vote, despite markets  fully pricing a rate hike. The decision reflected concerns about economic uncertainty and the need to assess the effects of previous tightening. Nevertheless, the central bank maintained a tightening bias. Inflation remains above its 1–3% target range. Higher fuel prices and broader underlying inflation pressures could require further tightening. The latest increase in geopolitical tensions adds to those risks.

The ECB has also seen a slightly hawkish repricing amid increasingly hawkish communication from policymakers. ECB's Žigman recently described another December rate hike as a strong possibility, while cautioning that policymakers need more data before deciding. The risk of higher energy prices feeding into wages and broader price-setting behaviour remains a major concern.

The BoJ hawkish repricing was driven by the central bank's increasingly confident assessment that underlying inflation has reached its 2% target. Governor Ueda recently signalled that the December meeting would include a discussion about another rate increase, suggesting that the BoJ is becoming more comfortable with further policy normalisation.

Additionally, Japanese inflation is expected to remain above the 2% target, partly reflecting the impact of higher energy prices. However, there are still divisions within the BoJ, with some policymakers warning that further tightening could undermine the economic recovery.

The BoC is also expected to deliver another rate hike at the next meeting, despite the economic data showing no urgency whatsoever. The central bank already delivered its first rate hike since July 2023 in October, taking the policy rate to 2.5%. The decision reflected a deterioration in the inflation outlook, with persistently elevated oil prices and increasing evidence that businesses will eventually pass higher input costs on to consumers.

The BoC also revised its inflation outlook higher, expecting CPI inflation to average around 3% in 2027. Governor Macklem acknowledged that further tightening might be necessary, although policymakers intend to proceed cautiously given the uncertainty surrounding the economic outlook.

The SNB remains the least hawkish central bank given limited signs of acceleration in underlying inflation. Although headline inflation has increased due to higher energy prices, the SNB has previously stressed that medium-term inflationary pressures remain relatively contained. There is also little evidence of second-round effects, meaning higher energy prices have not yet translated into broader and more persistent inflation. The Swiss franc's safe-haven appeal amid geopolitical uncertainty also provides some additional protection against imported inflation.

For the RBA markets are not pricing any more rate hikes, on the contrary, traders started to price in rate cuts in 2027. The RBA already raised rates 5 times this year, taking the Cash Rate from 3.60% to 4.85% in November. At the last meeting, three members voted against the increase as the appetite for further tightening faded amid softer labour market data.

The central bank's latest projections suggest inflation will remain above 3% until late 2027, but restrictive monetary policy is also expected to weigh on economic activity. Traders expect that the cumulative impact of previous tightening will eventually weaken growth enough to allow rate cuts.

TITRE : Goldman Sachs: We can explain almost the entire US inflation overshoot by tariffs and the war in Iran
DATE  : 13-11-2026 14:52 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/goldman-sachs-we-can-explain-almost-the-entire-us-inflation-overshoot-by-tariffs-and-the-war-in-iran/
================================================================================

Goldman Sachs is out with a chart that says the US inflation problem is almost entirely two things: tariffs and the Iran energy shock. Take those out and inflation is basically at target.

The latest hard data is ugly. September PCE was 3.6% y/y on headline and 3.1% on core, with gasoline and other energy goods up 9.2% in the month alone. Goldman sees headline peaking just shy of 3.9% right about now and core topping out near 3.3%.

Strip out the two shocks and the picture changes:

That’s the case for looking through it. Tariffs are a one-time level shift and oil doesn’t rise 30% every year. Hiking rates doesn’t reopen Hormuz.

Here’s the catch: Goldman’s own economists don’t think the Fed will look through it. After Tuesday’s hot October CPI, the third straight upside surprise, they added a hike to their forecast. They now see 25 bps in December and another 25 bps in March, and they pushed the first cut back to December 2027. A month ago they were arguing the Fed might be one-and-done after September.

The rest of the Street is moving faster. Bank of America is just out with a call for hikes in December, January and March, adding January to its forecast. Deutsche Bank has the same three. The market is pricing 86 bps of tightening over the year ahead, which is more than three hikes and more than any of them.

So Goldman is in an odd spot. It says the overshoot is temporary, and it’s still the dove on the Street with two more hikes.

There are a few holes in the temporary story too. First, look at the red line on the core chart. It bottomed near 2.0% in mid-2026 and has climbed to about 2.4% since. That’s the underlying trend moving the wrong way, even after Goldman takes out everything it can. Second, core services ex-housing hit 3.6% y/y in September, tied for the hottest since 2024. Neither tariffs nor crude explains that directly.

Third, “temporary” is carrying a heavy load. Tariff passthrough is in its second year and the energy wedge doesn’t fully close until late 2027. That’s nearly three years of above-target inflation on the heels of 2021-23. Expectations don’t care what the decomposition says, and neither does a Fed that had two dissenters asking for a hike in October.

Goldman is probably right on the math. But the Fed is hiking anyway, and the only debate left is how many.

Also, keep an eye on rates, the long end of the Treasury curve is starting to fret again, with US 10-year yields up another 6 bps to 5.16% today.
