The ECB looks set to take a breather this week, letting higher bond yields do some of its work, while December shapes up as the meeting where the next hike gets decided.

Summary:

The European Central Bank is widely expected to keep its deposit rate unchanged at 2.50% on Thursday, pausing after September’s rate increase as policymakers weigh an uncertain economic outlook against inflation that is still running well above target.

Morgan Stanley analysts said in a note that there is so far no sign of significant indirect or second-round effects from the latest energy shock on inflation, and that the recent rise in long-term bond yields is already providing a degree of additional tightening. ING economists argue that the French fiscal crisis and higher yields are already tightening financial conditions, making a hold very likely and pushing any further decision to December. Investors will focus on President Christine Lagarde’s press conference for any signals on the prospect of further rate increases in the coming months. The decision is due at 13:15 GMT (09:15 ET), with the press conference following at 13:45 GMT (09:45 ET).

The data calendar is busy. Germany’s Ifo business survey for October is out on Monday, the GfK consumer confidence survey follows on Wednesday, and eurozone business and consumer surveys are due on Thursday. On Friday, provisional third-quarter eurozone GDP figures will be released alongside flash October inflation data from individual countries, including France and Germany, offering an early read on whether the case for a December hike is strengthening.

Summary:

The board raised the rate from 1% in a 7-2 vote in September. Earlier:

---

Oil and a sliding kiwi have tipped most of NZIER’s Shadow Board toward another RBNZ hike, though the minority wants the central bank to let its earlier tightening do the work.

Summary:

A majority of the New Zealand Institute of Economic Research’s Monetary Policy Shadow Board has recommended that the Reserve Bank of New Zealand raise the Official Cash Rate by 25 basis points at its upcoming October Monetary Policy Review, which would take the rate to 3% from 2.75%. The group, which is independent of the central bank, said renewed pressure on global energy prices and a recent fall in the New Zealand dollar have increased the upside risks to inflation enough to justify another increase now.

The board was not unanimous. Several members argued that the RBNZ should give the increases already delivered more time to work through an economy that remains soft, with a cyclically weak labour market. One bank chief economist said there is a case to slow the pace of tightening, noting that while the short-term inflation picture looks more threatening, the medium-term outlook has not shifted, and that business and consumer surveys show no sign yet of second-round price effects.

Those favouring a hike pointed to a combination of pressures. Members cited the rebound in oil prices, a weaker currency, stronger-than-expected growth and core inflation that has stayed elevated, with one arguing that looking through these shocks has not returned inflation sustainably to the RBNZ’s 1% to 3% target band. Another member framed the decision as finely balanced, warning that with the conflict in Iran continuing, fuel-driven price pressures may prove less temporary than hoped.

A further view urged the bank to deliver the move and then signal a prolonged pause, arguing that clarity on the end of the tightening cycle would do more for business confidence than leaving the threat of further increases hanging over the economy.

Looking further out, most members agreed the OCR should rise over the coming year, with views of where it should sit in twelve months ranging between 3% and 4%. Some said the pace of further tightening should depend on how underlying inflation, inflation expectations and the recovery evolve. Several members flagged upcoming inflation data as important, and that release is likely to shape whether the RBNZ moves now or waits until year-end, as many commentators expect.

---

The New Zealand Institute of Economic Research (NZIER) is an independent economic research and consulting organisation based in Wellington, and its Monetary Policy Shadow Board is a panel of economists, academics and business leaders that meets ahead of each Reserve Bank of New Zealand decision. The key distinction is that the board says what the RBNZ should do, not what it will do. Each member assigns percentage weights to the policy options they prefer, and NZIER averages those preferences into a collective view. That makes the result a policy recommendation rather than a forecast or preview of the likely outcome. The two can differ: this month most members favour a hike, even though, as one member noted, many commentators expect the RBNZ to pause until year-end. NZIER says the exercise is also meant to encourage informed debate, show how a board structure might work, and explore how members could use probabilities to express uncertainty. The board is independent of the RBNZ, and members’ views are their own, not those of their employers.

The Governor describing the labour market as a little tight, plus staff’s downbeat view of supply capacity, leans hawkish and supports pricing for further tightening rather than a pause. Yesterday:

---

The RBA has never been chattier, but Westpac’s Luci Ellis says traders hunting for promises should listen instead to how it reads the data, because that is where the real signal lives.

Summary:

The Reserve Bank of Australia is talking more often, in more places and on more technical subjects than it did a few years ago, but it still is not promising anything about the path of interest rates, according to Westpac Group chief economist Luci Ellis. In a note on RBA communication, she argues that the clearest signal of future policy lies in how officials interpret incoming data, rather than in any form of forward guidance.

Ellis counts 58 public engagements on the RBA’s speeches page so far this year, already matching the total for all of last year and above the 52 recorded in 2024. Delivery has shifted too, away from set-piece policy speeches toward semi-scripted fireside chats and interviews across a wide range of media, part of a broader effort to explain decisions to the general public at a time when rates are rising but households are not the main source of inflation.

That wider reach can confuse markets, Ellis says, because different audiences call for different tones. The post-meeting press conference is aimed at the public, so a Governor who sounds softer there may simply be acknowledging that policy is causing pain, much as a doctor warns that a treatment might hurt without stopping it. Where tone differs, she argues, pre-drafted material such as the minutes is a more reliable guide to the Board’s thinking than off-the-cuff answers.

The content has also become more technical. Officials increasingly discuss estimates of unobservable variables such as neutral interest rates and financial conditions, and a spot check of readability scores suggests recent speeches are harder to read than those from 2021. The RBA has also stressed that monetary policy works through several channels beyond mortgage repayments, though Ellis notes that if the cashflow channel is the smallest, as the Deputy Governor suggested, a small group of households is bearing a heavy cost for limited effect on inflation.

The practical takeaway is to read the RBA’s interpretation of data. Ellis highlights that the Governor described the labour market as a little tight after the September meeting, even though the Board’s statement did not, and says the Deputy Governor’s recent hawkish tone was no surprise given staff’s downbeat view of supply capacity. With Senate Estimates coinciding with the quarterly CPI release ahead of the November decision, there will be plenty for markets to parse.
