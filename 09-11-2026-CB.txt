Hunter’s description of AI as a near-term inflationary force supports the RBA’s tightening bias rather than pointing to relief. That underpins the Australian dollar on rate differentials and keeps the focus on the risk of further hikes. Her financial stability warning adds a central bank voice to growing market unease over AI valuations and funding, at a time when tech-linked assets are already under strain. Any further move toward repricing would favour defensive positioning in AI-exposed equities. It could also limit the Australian dollar’s upside if global risk sentiment sours.

---

Last week:

---

The RBA sees AI as an inflation problem today and a potential financial stability problem tomorrow, with little sign yet of the productivity payoff that would justify the investment boom.

Summary:

Reserve Bank of Australia Assistant Governor Sarah Hunter has warned that artificial intelligence could pose growing risks to financial stability. She said some asset prices would be exposed to sharp repricing, or even disorderly adjustments, if returns on AI investment fall short of expectations.

Speaking at a UBS conference in Sydney on Monday, Hunter said there was limited evidence so far that AI had materially lifted aggregate productivity, and little sign it had caused large effects on employment across advanced economies, according to Reuters.

On inflation, her view was that AI’s impact would change over time. In the near term, she said, the technology acts as a positive shock to demand at a time when the economy’s supply capacity is constrained, which adds upward pressure on prices. Over the longer run, she expects supply to catch up as data centre capacity and related industries expand, easing bottlenecks. If AI then delivers broader productivity gains, the initial inflationary pressure from the investment boom is likely to fade, leaving the long-run effect broadly neutral.

The comments carry weight for the policy outlook. A surge in domestic data centre investment has been one of the forces adding to demand in the Australian economy. It is among the reasons the RBA has raised its cash rate five times this year, taking it to an 18-year high of 4.85%. Hunter’s description of AI as a near-term inflationary force fits with that tightening rather than pointing to any early relief.

Hunter also noted tentative signs of adjustment in the labour market. Job advertisements and employment outcomes have been weaker in occupations more exposed to AI, while growth has been stronger in areas where the technology has had less impact, particularly for younger workers.

Her financial stability warning lands at a sensitive moment for global markets, with investors already scrutinising the scale of AI spending and the debt and long-term commitments being taken on to fund it. Hunter stopped short of predicting a correction, framing the risk as conditional on investment returns disappointing.

For traders, the takeaway is a central bank that sees AI adding to inflation now while keeping an eye on the risk of a sharp reversal in the asset prices tied to it.

TITRE : Recap - BOJ’s Ueda sees inflation near 2% but flags upside risks as energy costs bite
DATE  : 08-11-2026 23:45 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/recap-boj-s-ueda-sees-inflation-near-2-but-flags-upside-risks-as-energy-costs-bite/
================================================================================

Ueda’s acknowledgement of upside inflation risks, with energy costs set to push price growth higher into early next year, leans hawkish. It supports expectations that the BOJ will keep normalising policy, which is mildly supportive for the yen and keeps upward pressure on Japanese government bond yields. His comment that the BOJ watches other central banks closely, including their effect on exchange rates, keeps yen moves on the policy radar. Georgieva’s warning on AI-related volatility adds to a theme already weighing on global tech this week.

---Ueda is comfortable that Japan has reached 2% underlying inflation, but with energy costs feeding through and upside risks building, the BOJ’s job has shifted from getting there to holding the line.

Summary:

I was away from the desk on Friday, so I’m catching up on a few items worth revisiting. First up are comments from Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda, who said the central bank expects underlying inflation to stay around its 2% target but sees some upside risks.

Speaking at a seminar hosted by the International Monetary Fund in Washington, Ueda said higher energy-related prices were starting to feed into consumer inflation and that price growth was likely to accelerate further into early next year, Reuters reported. He described Japan as being in an equilibrium in which wages are growing at a pace consistent with 2% inflation.

Even so, Ueda said the 2% target had yet to become a social norm in Japan, and that the BOJ’s goal now is to anchor underlying inflation around that level. Having reached it, the task is to keep it there.

He said the BOJ watches the actions of other central banks very closely because they influence asset prices, including exchange rates and interest rates, and reveal how policymakers elsewhere view their own economies. On unconventional tools, he said they had some positive effects when used but were not perfect substitutes for cutting rates from positive levels. The BOJ may have to use them again depending on the severity of any future shock. In that case, he said, it should look to limit side effects, including on market functioning.

Asked about the debate over central bank communication prompted by new Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, Ueda said a balance was needed between explaining policy goals and avoiding moral hazard in markets.

On artificial intelligence, Ueda said rapid advances had delivered a positive demand shock to Japan’s economy, while supply-side effects still needed study. He suggested that Japan, with its severe labour shortages, could benefit more than some countries from AI replacing labour. He also flagged concern about cyber risks to financial stability, saying some small banks and firms had not done enough to protect themselves.

IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, speaking at the same event, noted recent weakness in AI-related stocks and said central bankers would need to weigh any slowdown in AI-driven growth. She warned that concerns over dependence on the AI investment boom and high valuations were affecting markets globally. The resulting volatility, she said, would complicate central banks’ work at a time of rising risks to price stability.

For markets, the takeaway is a BOJ governor who sees inflation risks tilted higher, keeping the focus on the bank’s next policy steps and the yen.

TITRE : Markets price three more BoE hikes, but analysts see a shallower path after move to 4%
DATE  : 09-11-2026 00:47 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/markets-price-three-more-boe-hikes-but-analysts-see-a-shallower-path-after-move-to-4/
================================================================================

The gap between futures pricing and analyst expectations is the key tension for UK assets. If the BoE delivers only one more hike, front-end gilt yields have room to fall and the curve could rally, which is why several analysts favour adding duration. For sterling, a shallower path than priced is a mild headwind on rate differentials. Analysts see little chance of the BoE out-tightening its peers, which limits the pound’s upside. Oil is the swing factor: a sustained rise on Iran escalation, as this weekend’s headlines threaten, would push the BoE back toward the market’s hawkish pricing.

---

---

The BoE has bought insurance against an energy-driven inflation spiral, but analysts broadly think markets are overpaying for further protection, with one more hike looking more likely than three.

Summary:

Markets are pricing three more Bank of England rate hikes by the end of next year, but analysts reacting to last week’s move increasingly doubt the central bank will need to go that far. Most see one further increase as the more likely path.

The BoE raised Bank Rate by 25 basis points to 4% in a unanimous decision, aiming to contain inflation that is running at 3.5% and still rising. The move was widely expected and caused little disruption in markets, but analysts said the unanimity could reinforce expectations of further tightening.

The driver is the energy shock from the war with Iran. Policymakers are concerned that higher energy costs will pass through to consumers and become embedded in the economy, and they are using higher borrowing costs to cool demand. One forecast sees inflation peaking later and higher than previously thought, at around 4.5% next summer, as energy prices continue to grind higher. The key question, analysts said, is whether this imported shock stays contained or spills into domestic wages and services prices.

Several analysts characterised the decision as a risk-management or insurance hike rather than the start of an aggressive cycle. In this view, the Bank is acting to protect its credibility and limit second-round effects, even though evidence of energy costs becoming embedded in wider wages and prices remains limited. Resilient growth, consumer spending and labour market data have given it both a reason to hike and the room to do so.

That has opened a gap between market pricing and economists’ expectations. Futures imply significantly more tightening than most forecasters anticipate. Higher bond yields are already doing some of the Bank’s work, and higher energy bills, mortgage rates and tighter financial conditions are expected to weigh on household spending. One analyst said the move could prove “one and done” if energy prices ease before February, while others see one further hike, most likely in February, as the base case.

The outlook remains closely tied to the Middle East. A prolonged conflict that keeps oil prices elevated into 2027 would strengthen the case for further tightening. Analysts flagged a return to US strikes on Iran, or rising domestic political uncertainty, as risks to a shallower path.

For investors, a number of analysts see value in gilts after the rise in yields. Some favour the seven- to eight-year part of the curve and gradually extending duration, arguing that much of the UK’s fiscal and supply risk is already priced. For sterling, the key issue is whether the BoE ends up tightening much more than other major central banks, which analysts view as unlikely for now.
