================================================================================

The poll firms up December as the likely window for the BOJ’s next move, which should limit yen sensitivity to the 30 October meeting but raises the stakes around the December decision. The bigger currency risk may be the vote itself: with the yen only modestly above its 2026 low, a repeat of September’s dissent from government-nominated board members could be read as political resistance to tightening and trigger fresh selling. Rising energy and import costs tied to the Middle East war keep oil a direct input into Japan’s inflation outlook, so a further jump in crude would likely harden the case for hikes. For JGBs, the shift toward a 2% or higher terminal rate, combined with fiscal concerns, points to continued pressure on yields.

-

Economists now broadly expect the BOJ to keep raising rates every quarter, with the main risk to the yen coming not from the timing of the next move but from who votes against it.

Summary:

The Bank of Japan is expected to raise its benchmark interest rate to 1.50% in December, according to a Reuters poll of economists, extending a pattern of quarterly increases after hikes in June and September.

Of 78 economists surveyed between 13 and 19 October, 54, or nearly 70%, expected a move to 1.50% this quarter, almost double the share in the September poll. About 80% of those naming a month chose December, while just 2% expected action at the BOJ’s two-day meeting ending on 30 October.

Higher energy and import costs, made worse by a weak yen and the war in the Middle East, are adding to inflation pressure, the poll found. A period of relative stability in the yen, which faced heavy selling for much of the year and prompted intervention from both Japanese authorities and the US Treasury, also supports the case for a December move. Board member Junko Koeda said last week the BOJ should not become overly cautious about raising rates, now at their highest in 31 years, given significant upside risks to inflation.

Not every economist sees the need for speed. Some argued the economy is not overheating enough to justify back-to-back hikes and that prices remain within the BOJ’s expected range. Sources have told Reuters that many BOJ officials want more data on how earlier increases have affected financial conditions.

Expectations for the peak rate have shifted higher. Sixty per cent of respondents saw the policy rate at 1.75% by the end of June next year, while the share expecting it to reach 2.00% or more rose to 51% from 40% in September.

Politics remains a risk for the yen. In September, two board members nominated by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, who favour looser fiscal policy, voted against raising rates to 1.25%. Nearly three-quarters of economists said further dissent at the next hike could weigh on the yen, which sits only about 4% above its 2026 low near 164 per dollar.

On fiscal policy, nearly two-thirds of respondents expected the government to keep new bond issuance below 40 trillion yen, about $250 billion, for fiscal 2027, after Takaichi said she aims to hold issuance around that level.

With the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank also expected to raise rates in December, according to separate Reuters polls, the BOJ’s next decision will be judged as much on the vote split as on the move itself.

Flagging a higher-than-expected trimmed mean a week before the official CPI release is an unusually direct steer, and it shifts the risk balance for 28 October toward an upside surprise. Combined with his view that capacity pressures are tighter than the RBA assumed, the remarks are likely to reinforce hike pricing for 3 November, which would tend to support the Australian dollar and lift short-end Australian yields. His comment on supply shocks matters for the oil channel: it suggests the RBA is less inclined to look through energy-driven price rises while expectations are at risk, making crude a more direct input into Australian rate pricing. Softer labour data gives the doves a counterweight, but Hauser’s framing suggests it is not enough on its own to keep the Board on hold.

---

RBA 2026 dates, next meeting in just a couple of weeks time:

-

Hauser has all but previewed a hot underlying inflation print, and with capacity tighter than assumed and supply shocks no longer something to look through, a softer jobs market looks like a footnote rather than a reprieve.

Summary:

More from Reserve Bank of Australia Deputy Governor Andrew Hauser, who signalled that underlying inflation for the September quarter is likely to come in above the central bank’s expectations, adding to his earlier comments that the inflation outlook looks worse than forecast.

Speaking at Citi’s Australia and New Zealand Investment Conference in Sydney, Hauser said the quarterly trimmed mean appeared set to be higher than the RBA had expected. The trimmed mean strips out the largest price rises and falls each quarter to give a clearer read on underlying inflation, and it is the measure the RBA watches most closely. His remark comes a week before the Australian Bureau of Statistics publishes September-quarter inflation data on 28 October.

Hauser also said capacity pressures in the economy were more elevated than the RBA had anticipated, suggesting demand is still running further ahead of supply than policymakers assumed.

On supply shocks, Hauser said the RBA has to respond to them in order to keep inflation expectations anchored. Central banks often look through supply-driven price rises on the basis that they fade over time, but his comments suggest the RBA sees less room to do so after a long period of high inflation. The RBA has previously pointed to the Middle East conflict and its effect on oil prices as a supply-side pressure.

He added that monetary policy cannot target current inflation, since it works with a lag, but stressed that this does not mean current inflation is irrelevant, as persistent price rises can feed into expectations.

The labour market offered a partial offset. Hauser said September’s employment report was clearly a little weaker than expected and that while unemployment remains quite low, conditions are easing.

The RBA lifted its cash rate to 4.60% on 29 September. The Board meets on 2 and 3 November, with the decision due at 2.30pm Sydney time on 3 November (03:30 GMT, 22:30 ET on 2 November), alongside updated staff forecasts. A trimmed mean result above expectations next week would likely leave a further rate rise firmly on the table.

TITRE : PBOC is expected to set the USD/CNY reference rate at 6.6779 – Reuters estimate
DATE  : 21-10-2026 00:24 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/pboc-is-expected-to-set-the-usd-cny-reference-rate-at-6-6779-reuters-estimate/
================================================================================

The fixing mechanism matters most right now because its message has shifted over the past month. For nearly a year, the PBOC has set its daily guidance weaker than market expectations, which traders read as an effort to slow the yuan's appreciation, and in late August that gap reached its widest since February, shortly after the yuan touched a three-and-a-half-year high. Through October, however, the midpoint has strengthened at a faster pace, narrowing its gap with market expectations and suggesting the central bank has eased its resistance to yuan gains. The fixing is still set weaker than models imply, so this looks like a loosening of the brakes rather than their removal. The balance Beijing is weighing is unchanged: a firmer currency supports capital stability and import costs, but too rapid a rise risks eroding export competitiveness while the domestic economy remains soft. The gap between each day's fixing and consensus estimates remains one of the clearest guides to how far the PBOC is willing to let the yuan run.

---

The daily fixing is Beijing's quietest but clearest tool for telling markets how fast it wants the yuan to move, and after months of saying slow down, it has lately been saying go, gradually.

Summary:

The People's Bank of China sets the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT, a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets. It has taken on added significance in recent weeks as policymakers have shifted from restraining the yuan's rise to guiding it higher.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the yuan is allowed to trade within a band of plus or minus 2% around the central midpoint set each trading day. That midpoint reflects a mix of inputs, including the previous day's closing price, movements in major currencies such as the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic considerations like capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The calculation is not purely mechanical, giving policymakers room to use the fixing to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is set, onshore USD/CNY trades freely within the allowable range. Should market pressure push the currency toward either edge of that band, the central bank can step in to smooth volatility, whether through direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance channelled through state-owned banks. As a result, the daily fixing is often read as a policy signal rather than a purely technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected midpoint typically suggests the PBOC is leaning against depreciation, while a weaker fixing can signal tolerance for a softer currency, or unease with how quickly the yuan is rising.

That second reading has dominated for much of the past year. For nearly a year, the central bank set its daily guidance weaker than market expectations, a pattern traders and analysts interpreted as an attempt to slow the yuan's appreciation. The gap reached its widest since February in late August, shortly after the yuan touched a three-and-a-half-year high against the dollar.

September has brought a clear change of tone. The midpoint has strengthened at a faster pace this month, narrowing its gap with market expectations and suggesting the PBOC has eased its resistance to yuan gains. The central bank strengthened the fixing for eight consecutive sessions, the longest streak since 2023, and the midpoint has since reached its strongest level since February 2023. The onshore yuan has traded at its strongest since January 2023, while the offshore rate has reached levels last seen in July 2022. Even so, the fixing is still being set well weaker than market estimates, with one recent midpoint more than 500 pips below a Reuters survey forecast, a reminder that Beijing is allowing appreciation on its own terms rather than stepping aside.

The timing has been linked to diplomacy.

The underlying tension is a familiar one. A firmer yuan supports capital stability and eases import costs, but a rapid rise risks undermining export competitiveness at a moment when China's broader economy continues to show signs of softness. For traders and investors, the gap between each day's fixing and consensus estimates remains one of the clearest windows into how Beijing is weighing that trade-off, and whether the recent green light for gains stays in place once the summit has passed.

TITRE : Japan’s Katayama sees no gap with BOJ, says wage-driven inflation is near
DATE  : 21-10-2026 00:54 UTC
URL   : https://investinglive.com/central-banks/japan-s-katayama-sees-no-gap-with-boj-says-wage-driven-inflation-is-near/
================================================================================

Katayama’s comments may ease some of the political risk flagged in today’s Reuters poll, where most economists warned that dissent from government-nominated BOJ board members could weigh on the yen. Her description of an economy close to wage-driven inflation is language that supports the case for further tightening, which would tend to support the yen and keep upward pressure on short-dated JGB yields. Her reference to import-driven price rises also keeps oil in focus, with energy costs tied to the Middle East conflict a direct driver of Japan’s inflation. The tension between consumption tax cuts and BOJ tightening remains, and markets may test her “no gap” message against how board members vote at the next hike.

-

Katayama’s message was that fiscal support and monetary tightening can coexist, but the real test of “no gap” will come when the BOJ next votes.

Summary:

Japan’s Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama said on Wednesday that the economy is on the cusp of a shift to sustained inflation driven by rising wages, and that she sees no gap between the government’s views and those of the Bank of Japan.

Speaking in parliament, Katayama said Japan is currently experiencing price rises driven largely by higher import costs. She said the economy is close to moving into a period where inflation is sustained by wage growth, a transition the BOJ has long described as a condition for normalising monetary policy.

Katayama said the government’s planned consumption tax cut and household payouts are intended to cushion the impact of rising living costs. Asked by a lawmaker whether the tax cut could work against the central bank’s efforts to tame inflation through rate hikes, she said the government and the BOJ are exchanging views on various fronts and that she sees no gap between their positions.

The question reflects a tension markets have been watching closely. Fiscal measures that support household spending can add to demand at a time when the BOJ is tightening policy, and investors have been alert to signs of political resistance to further rate rises. In September, two board members nominated by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s government voted against the decision to raise rates to 1.25%, briefly weakening the yen.

A Reuters poll published on Wednesday found most economists expect the BOJ to raise its policy rate to 1.50% in December, keeping up a quarterly pace of increases. Nearly three-quarters said further dissenting votes at the next hike could weigh on the yen, which remains only modestly above its 2026 low.

Katayama’s remarks may help reassure markets that the government is not pushing against tightening, although she did not explicitly endorse further rate rises. Import costs, including energy prices tied to the Middle East conflict, remain a key driver of the inflation she described.

The BOJ’s next policy meeting ends on 30 October, when it will publish fresh growth and inflation forecasts. Attention will then shift to December, where the vote split may prove as important as the decision itself.
