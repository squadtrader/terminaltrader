Kent’s remarks pull in two directions, which may limit any lasting move in the Australian dollar. Endorsing the post-jobs repricing suggests the RBA is comfortable with markets scaling back near-term hike bets, a mild negative for the currency and supportive for short-end bonds. But the neutral rate argument is a hawkish undercurrent, since it implies the 4.60% cash rate may be doing less work than assumed. That makes the 28 October inflation data the pivotal event: a hot print would revive November hike pricing quickly, while a soft one would likely cement a pause. Rate-sensitive sectors, especially property and banks, will be tracking that shift closely.

Earlier:

---Kent is happy for markets to pare November hike bets after a weak jobs report, but his warning that neutral rates are rising leaves the RBA plenty of room to tighten again if inflation runs hot.

Summary:

Reserve Bank of Australia Assistant Governor Christopher Kent said there is reasonable evidence that neutral interest rates are rising, both in Australia and overseas, raising the question of whether monetary policy is as tight as it appears, even after four rate increases this year.

Speaking at a fireside chat at the Commonwealth Bank’s Global Markets Conference in Sydney, Kent, who oversees financial markets at the central bank, said he was confident policy is somewhat restrictive but questioned whether it is restrictive enough.

The neutral rate is the level of interest rates that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy. If it has drifted higher, a cash rate that looks tight on paper may be exerting less pressure on demand and inflation than policymakers had assumed, which would strengthen the case for keeping rates higher for longer or tightening further.

Kent’s remarks come as the RBA weighs its next move. The board lifted the cash rate by 25 basis points to 4.60% on 29 September, its fourth increase of 2026, citing inflation that remains too high, energy prices pushed up by the broadening conflict in the Middle East, and lingering capacity pressures. It said further increases were possible if needed.

On the economy, Kent said household demand is softening, while business credit growth remains strong. He described recent tax changes affecting housing as helpful for monetary policy, a sign the bank sees fiscal settings working with it rather than against it on housing demand.

He was more cautious on the labour market. Kent said he was wary of reading too much into monthly employment figures, but acknowledged conditions have become less tight. Data last week showed employment fell by about 39,000 in September, against expectations of a gain of around 20,000, with the unemployment rate rising to 4.7%, the highest in around five years.

That report sent the Australian dollar and short-term interest rates lower as traders pared bets on a near-term hike. Kent said that market reaction made sense, a comment likely to be read as the bank seeing no reason to push back against the repricing.

Even so, the debate is far from settled. ANZ kept its call for a further 25 basis point increase in November after the jobs data, arguing that upward revisions to the bank’s inflation forecasts make a hike more likely than not.

The next key test is quarterly inflation data on 28 October, ahead of the RBA’s 2 to 3 November meeting. Kent’s comments on a rising neutral rate suggest that, if inflation surprises to the upside, the bank still sees room to tighten.

Oil is a central channel for Japan: as a heavy energy importer, it feels Middle East price spikes directly, and higher crude both lifts inflation and widens the trade bill. That cuts both ways for the yen. By framing AI demand and the labour market as genuine demand pressures, Ueda gives hawks a stronger footing, which could support Japanese government bond yields at the short end. Ueda is quietly making the case that Japan’s inflation is no longer just an imported energy story, and that shift is what would ultimately justify the BoJ’s next hike.

Summary:

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda said it is becoming more important to anchor underlying inflation around the central bank’s 2% target, setting out a case in which both supply shocks and strong demand are now pushing prices higher in Japan.

In remarks reported early on Monday, Asian time, Ueda said Japan is seeing a positive cycle in which wages are rising and prices are recovering. He then distinguished between two sources of price pressure. On the supply side, he said the conflict in the Middle East is lifting energy costs and a range of other prices. On the demand side, he said very strong global demand linked to artificial intelligence is boosting domestic capital spending, exports and the prices of various goods.

Ueda added that Japan’s moderate economic recovery and a tight labour market are also adding to inflation.

The distinction matters for policy. Central banks typically look through supply-driven inflation, such as an energy price spike, on the basis that it should fade. Demand-driven inflation, by contrast, is the kind monetary policy is designed to address. By highlighting AI-related demand and a tight jobs market alongside the energy shock, Ueda appears to be building the argument that recent price gains are not purely temporary.

The comments build on remarks late last week, when Ueda said the BoJ would continue to raise interest rates as underlying inflation approaches 2%, and that the timing and pace of increases would depend on exchange rates, AI-driven demand and developments in the Middle East. He also said policymakers would discuss at their next meeting whether to revise their assessment of underlying inflation.

The BoJ’s short-term policy rate stands at 1.25%, and the bank’s next decision is due on 30 October. Market pricing has leaned against a move at that meeting, but language stressing the need to anchor inflation keeps the prospect of a hike in the near future firmly in view.

The yen edged slightly higher after the comments.

The key question for markets is whether the BoJ formally upgrades its view of underlying inflation at the October meeting. Such a shift would signal that the conditions for the next rate rise are falling into place, even if the bank chooses to wait.

The fixing mechanism matters most right now because its message has shifted over the past month. For nearly a year, the PBOC has set its daily guidance weaker than market expectations, which traders read as an effort to slow the yuan's appreciation, and in late August that gap reached its widest since February, shortly after the yuan touched a three-and-a-half-year high. The daily fixing is Beijing's quietest but clearest tool for telling markets how fast it wants the yuan to move, and after months of saying slow down, it has lately been saying go, gradually.

Summary:

The People's Bank of China sets the daily USD/CNY reference rate at around 0115 GMT, a fixing that remains one of the most closely watched signals in Asian foreign exchange markets. It has taken on added significance in recent weeks as policymakers have shifted from restraining the yuan's rise to guiding it higher.

China operates a managed floating exchange rate system, under which the yuan is allowed to trade within a band of plus or minus 2% around the central midpoint set each trading day. That midpoint reflects a mix of inputs, including the previous day's closing price, movements in major currencies such as the US dollar, broader international FX conditions, and domestic considerations like capital flows, growth momentum and financial stability objectives. The calculation is not purely mechanical, giving policymakers room to use the fixing to guide market expectations.

Once the midpoint is set, onshore USD/CNY trades freely within the allowable range. Should market pressure push the currency toward either edge of that band, the central bank can step in to smooth volatility, whether through direct buying or selling of yuan, adjustments to liquidity conditions, or guidance channelled through state-owned banks. As a result, the daily fixing is often read as a policy signal rather than a purely technical reference point. A stronger-than-expected midpoint typically suggests the PBOC is leaning against depreciation, while a weaker fixing can signal tolerance for a softer currency, or unease with how quickly the yuan is rising.

That second reading has dominated for much of the past year. For nearly a year, the central bank set its daily guidance weaker than market expectations, a pattern traders and analysts interpreted as an attempt to slow the yuan's appreciation. The gap reached its widest since February in late August, shortly after the yuan touched a three-and-a-half-year high against the dollar.

September has brought a clear change of tone. The midpoint has strengthened at a faster pace this month, narrowing its gap with market expectations and suggesting the PBOC has eased its resistance to yuan gains. The central bank strengthened the fixing for eight consecutive sessions, the longest streak since 2023, and the midpoint has since reached its strongest level since February 2023. The onshore yuan has traded at its strongest since January 2023, while the offshore rate has reached levels last seen in July 2022. Even so, the fixing is still being set well weaker than market estimates, with one recent midpoint more than 500 pips below a Reuters survey forecast, a reminder that Beijing is allowing appreciation on its own terms rather than stepping aside.

The timing has been linked to diplomacy.

The underlying tension is a familiar one. A firmer yuan supports capital stability and eases import costs, but a rapid rise risks undermining export competitiveness at a moment when China's broader economy continues to show signs of softness. For traders and investors, the gap between each day's fixing and consensus estimates remains one of the clearest windows into how Beijing is weighing that trade-off, and whether the recent green light for gains stays in place once the summit has passed.

The yen’s muted response suggests markets saw little new here beyond confirmation of the BoJ’s existing approach. For Japanese government bonds, Ueda’s message cuts two ways: he is not offering a backstop at any particular yield level, yet he is signalling the bank will act if the selloff becomes disorderly. Super-long JGBs, the most sensitive part of the curve, are where any test of that commitment would show up first. Ueda is telling bond markets that the best protection against a disorderly selloff is a BoJ that keeps raising rates on time, not a promise to step in at a set level.

Summary:

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda said the central bank’s basic approach to reducing its bond purchases is unchanged, and declined to say in advance when it might step into the government bond market with emergency operations.

Speaking in the same appearance in which he stressed the importance of anchoring underlying inflation around 2%, Ueda said the BoJ would look comprehensively at market conditions before deciding whether to intervene. He pointed to two considerations in particular: whether long-term interest rates are rising at a rapid pace, and whether bond market functioning is showing signs of instability.

Ueda also set out how he expects short and long-term rates to interact as policy tightens. If the BoJ raises its short-term policy rate, he said, long-term rates are likely to rise steadily in line with those steps. But if the policy rate is not adjusted appropriately, he warned, market concerns about the risk of inflation overshooting could push long-term yields higher.

In effect, Ueda argued that well-calibrated rate increases are a stabilising force for the Japanese government bond market rather than a threat to it. By conducting policy appropriately to achieve its 2% inflation goal, he said, the BoJ can help stabilise JGBs. He added that financial conditions remain accommodative, a reminder that policy is still supportive of growth even after the bank’s recent hikes.

The remarks address a tension that has run through the BoJ’s exit from years of ultra-loose policy. The bank has been gradually scaling back its bond buying while raising rates, leaving investors alert to the risk of disorderly moves in long-term yields. By declining to set a trigger for emergency buying, Ueda keeps the bank’s options open while signalling that intervention would be reserved for genuinely disorderly conditions rather than a steady repricing.

The comments also reinforce the hawkish tone of his earlier remarks, framing further rate increases as consistent with market stability. The BoJ’s policy rate stands at 1.25%, with the next decision due on 30 October.

The yen was little changed as Ueda spoke, suggesting the bond market comments largely confirmed existing expectations.
